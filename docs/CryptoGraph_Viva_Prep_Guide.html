      A. Money and markets

      Coin (cryptocurrency)
      Digital money bought and sold on the internet, such as Bitcoin (BTC) or Ethereum (ETH).We follow 30 coins, plus LUNC and FTT for two famous crashes.
      Exchange
      A marketplace where coins are traded, like a stock market that never closes: open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.Binance, the biggest; we use its free public price records.
      Stablecoin, USDT, peg and depeg
      A stablecoin is a coin built to always be worth one US dollar; USDT ("Tether") is the biggest. "Pegged" means held at $1; a depeg is when it slips away from $1, a sign of panic.Binance prices are in USDT; we flag the 4 days USDT slipped more than 0.5%.
      Candle (OHLCV)
      One hour of trading squeezed into five numbers: the price at the start (open), the highest (high), the lowest (low), the price at the end (close) and how much was traded (volume). Charts draw it like a little candle. Binance calls it a "kline".59,088 hours of candles, from January 2020 to September 2026.
      Return
      How much the price changed, as a percentage: $100 to $103 is a return of +3%.
      Log return
      The same idea written with logarithms: ln(103 ÷ 100) = 0.0296, almost the same as 3%. Its advantage: hour-by-hour log returns simply add up to the day's log return, which plain percentages do not (+10% then −10% leaves you at −1%, not 0).Every input starts from hourly log returns.
      Volume and relative volume
      Volume is how much was traded in an hour. Relative volume compares it with the coin's normal over the past week: "three times busier than usual".The biggest single reason behind our alerts (about 25%).
      Volatility
      How jumpy a price is. A calm coin moves 0.3% in a typical hour, a jumpy one 2%. Measured as the typical size of recent returns (their standard deviation, Part B), which is called realised volatility.Features rv_24h and rv_72h: jumpiness over the last 24 and 72 hours.
      Liquidity, thinly traded
      How easily you can trade without moving the price. In a thinly traded coin few trades happen, so its price updates late and can look as if it "follows" others.QNT, the one coin that seemed to follow ETH.
      Market capitalisation
      Price × number of coins in existence: the coin's total size.Our 30 coins are the biggest by market cap (CoinGecko's list) that trade on Binance with enough history.
      Crash, pump and shock
      A crash is a sudden big fall, a pump a sudden big rise. A shock is our word for an unusually big move in either direction for that coin, judged against its own recent jumpiness.A move bigger than 2.29 times the coin's typical hourly move.
      Correlation, co-movement
      Whether two coins go up and down together. Crypto coins are strongly linked: when Bitcoin falls, most coins fall.Average hourly correlation about 0.58 (1 = always together).
      Market factor, systematic and idiosyncratic
      The part of a move that all coins share ("the whole market moved") is systematic; the part that belongs to one coin alone ("its own news") is idiosyncratic.The shared part is 64% of hourly movement.
      Diversification
      Spreading money over many coins so one bad coin hurts less. It only works if coins do not all fall together, and in crises they do.
      Beta, "high beta"
      How strongly a coin swings when the market swings. A high-beta coin rises and falls more than the market.
      Contagion and interdependence
      Contagion: trouble spreading from one coin to others, beyond their usual link, like a yawn going round a room. Interdependence: they always move together anyway. Forbes and Rigobon (2002) warned that the two are easy to confuse.So we judge shocks against each coin's normal and only count shocks that come strictly later.
      Spillover, connectedness, transmitter and receiver
      Spillover: how much of one coin's surprise comes from other coins. Connectedness: how much spillover there is overall. A transmitter sends more than it receives; a receiver the opposite.Measured with the Diebold–Yilmaz method (Part 11).
      Systemic risk
      The risk that trouble in one place brings down the whole system, like one bank's failure causing queues at every bank.
      Leverage
      Trading with borrowed money. With 10× leverage, a 10% move doubles your stake or wipes it out.
      Futures, perpetual futures, funding rate, premium
      A future is a contract to buy or sell later at a price agreed now. Perpetual futures never expire; to keep their price close to the real price, one side pays the other a small fee every few hours, the funding rate. The premium is how far the futures price sits above the real price.We tried these as inputs; they made results worse.
      Liquidation and liquidation cascade
      When a leveraged trader's losses get too big, the exchange force-sells their position: a liquidation. Forced selling pushes prices down, which forces more selling, like dominoes: a cascade.10 October 2025.
      ETF
      An exchange-traded fund: a fund sold on the ordinary stock market that holds an asset. The US approved spot Bitcoin ETFs on 10 January 2024, so ordinary investors could buy Bitcoin through their usual broker.
      Sectors
      Groups of coins by what they do. Payments (XRP, XLM, LTC: sending money); smart-contract platforms (ETH, SOL, ADA: shared computers that run apps); DeFi (AAVE, UNI: banking apps with no bank); oracle (LINK: brings outside data such as prices onto blockchains); layer 2 (ARB, POL: faster, cheaper lanes built on top of Ethereum); meme coins (DOGE, SHIB, PEPE: driven by jokes and hype); exchange token (BNB, tied to Binance); privacy (ZEC: hides who paid whom); identity / AI (WLD).A hand-made list of 9 sectors in sectors.csv.
      Listing, delisting, ticker change
      Listing: an exchange starts trading a coin; delisting: it stops. A ticker is the coin's short name; MATIC was renamed POL in 2024, swapped one for one.FTT was delisted after FTX failed; XMR was delisted in February 2024, so we left it out.
      Survivorship bias
      Studying only the winners. We picked today's top 30, so coins that died along the way are missing, which can make the market look healthier than it was.
      Risk desk, regulator, early warning
      The people who would use this tool: an exchange's team that watches risk, a market supervisor, or a trader who wants a warning before trouble spreads.
      UTC and hour of the week
      UTC is world standard time (London time without summer time). An hour of the week is one of 168 slots, Monday 00:00 to Sunday 23:00.Wednesday 12:00 UTC is the jumpiest slot, Sunday 06:00 the calmest.

      B. Statistics

      Mean and median
      Mean: add everything up and divide. Median: the middle value when sorted; one crazy value cannot drag it around."Back to normal after a median of 8 to 10 hours".
      Standard deviation
      The typical distance from the average: how spread out numbers are. For returns, it is the volatility.
      z-score
      How many standard deviations a value is from normal. z = 3 means "three typical moves big".Our shock: |z| above 2.29 (|z| means ignore the sign).
      Percentile
      The value below which a given share of the data falls; the 99th percentile is bigger than 99% of values.
      Distribution, bell curve, fat tails
      A distribution says how often each size of move happens. In a bell curve (the "normal" distribution) extreme values are very rare. Fat tails: extreme values happen far more often than a bell curve says, as in crypto.
      Pearson and Spearman correlation
      Pearson uses the actual values; Spearman uses only the order (1st, 2nd, 3rd…), so it is safe from extreme values and good for comparing two rankings. Both run from −1 to +1.
      Regression and R²
      Regression fits a formula that predicts one number from others (often a straight line). R² is the share of the ups and downs the formula explains: 0 = nothing, 1 = everything.
      Null hypothesis and p-value
      A test starts from a boring assumption, the null hypothesis ("there is no effect"). The p-value says how surprising our data would be if the boring assumption were true. A small p (below 0.05) means "hard to explain by luck". It is not the chance that the effect is real.
      Significant, one-sided, two-sided
      Significant: unlikely to be luck. One-sided asks "is A better than B?"; two-sided asks "are A and B different, either way?".
      Confidence interval
      A range of believable values for the true answer. If the 95% interval for "TGN minus cascade" is 0.019 to 0.074, it does not include 0, so the TGN is reliably better.
      Bootstrap
      Checking how much a result could wobble by luck: draw the data again many times with replacement (like drawing marbles and putting each one back), recompute each time, and look at the spread.We draw whole days, 1,000 times.
      Null model
      Fake data built to have no real effect (shuffled or shifted in time), to see what chance alone produces.
      Multiple testing
      Run many tests and some will look significant by luck, like a streak of heads in many coin flips. Corrections such as Benjamini–Hochberg raise the bar.
      Base rate, positive rate, class imbalance
      How often the answer is "yes". At 1 hour only 10.5% of our events are "yes": the classes are imbalanced, so "always say no" is 89.5% accurate and useless.
      Stationary
      Behaves the same way over time. Returns are stationary (they bounce around zero); prices are not (they drift and grow).
      Lag and autocorrelation
      A lag is a step back in time: "lag 1" is one hour earlier. Autocorrelation is a series' correlation with its own past.

      C. Machine learning

      Model
      A recipe that turns inputs into a prediction. Learning means adjusting the recipe from examples.
      Feature (input) and label (target)
      Features are the facts the model sees (a coin's volatility, its volume…). The label is the right answer we want it to predict.Label: did coin j shock within k hours? 1 = yes, 0 = no.
      Classification and regression
      Classification predicts a category or a chance (shock or not); regression predicts a number (next hour's volatility).
      Training, validation and test sets
      Practice questions (training), a mock exam used to choose settings (validation), and the final exam, taken once (test).Split by date: to June 2022, July 2022 to December 2023, 2024 onwards.
      Data leakage and embargo
      Leakage: the model accidentally sees the future, which is cheating and gives fake good scores. An embargo is a gap between periods so nothing seeps across the border.7 days.
      Parameter and hyperparameter
      Parameters are the knobs the model sets itself while learning; hyperparameters are settings we choose (memory size, learning rate, number of epochs).The TGN has 70,021 parameters.
      Loss
      One number that says how wrong the model is. Training tries to make it smaller.
      Gradient and gradient descent
      The gradient says which way to turn each knob to reduce the loss, like feeling which way is downhill in fog. Gradient descent takes small steps downhill.
      Learning rate
      The size of each step: too big and you overshoot the valley, too small and you never arrive.0.0005 for the TGN.
      Optimizer (Adam)
      The rule that turns gradients into knob changes. Adam gives each knob its own step size that adapts as it learns.
      Batch and epoch
      A batch is a handful of examples processed together; an epoch is one full pass through all the training data.About 200 events per batch; up to 100 epochs.
      Overfitting, underfitting, generalisation
      Overfitting: memorising the practice questions; underfitting: not even learning those; generalisation: doing well on questions never seen.
      Regularisation
      Tricks that stop memorising, such as dropout and weight decay (Part D).
      Early stopping and patience
      Stop training when the mock-exam score has not improved for a while, and keep the best version. Patience is how long you wait.10 epochs.
      Seed
      The number that fixes a run's random start. Training with several seeds shows whether a result is luck.5 seeds, numbered 0 to 4.
      Baseline
      A simpler competitor that the main model must beat to be worth it.
      Ablation
      Removing one part of a model to see what that part was worth.The TGN without memory.
      Ensemble
      Combining the answers of several models, like asking several doctors.
      Decision tree and gradient boosting
      A decision tree reaches an answer through yes/no questions ("is volume unusually high?"). Gradient boosting adds hundreds of small trees, each fixing the mistakes of the ones before.
      Logistic regression
      The simplest learned model for chances: one weighted sum of the inputs, squeezed into a probability.
      Precision, recall, AUPRC, AUROC, lift
      Precision: of the alarms, how many were right. Recall: of the real events, how many we caught. AUPRC and AUROC sum up the whole trade-off in one number; lift is a score divided by the random score. All explained slowly in Part 5.
      Threshold and calibration
      A threshold is the cut-off where a chance becomes an alert. Calibration makes chances honest: things given 30% should happen about 30% of the time.

      D. Deep learning

      Neuron and neural network
      A neuron takes some numbers, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up, and bends the result. A neural network is many neurons in layers, each layer feeding the next.
      Weights and bias
      The knobs (parameters). A weight says how much an input matters; the bias shifts the result up or down.
      Layer, linear layer, MLP
      A layer is a row of neurons. A linear (or "fully connected") layer links every input to every neuron. An MLP (multi-layer perceptron) is a small stack of such layers.The TGN's prediction head is an MLP: 161 numbers in, 64 in the middle, 5 out.
      Activation function, ReLU
      The bend after the sum. Without bends, many layers collapse into one straight line. ReLU is the simplest bend: keep positive numbers, turn negatives into 0.
      Logit and sigmoid
      The logit is the raw score from the last layer, any number from very negative to very positive. The sigmoid squeezes it into a chance between 0 and 1 (a logit of 0 means 50%).The TGN outputs 5 logits, one per window.
      Vector, dimension, tensor
      A vector is a list of numbers; "64-dimensional" means a list of 64 numbers. A tensor is a block of numbers in any number of directions, such as 200 events × 5 numbers.
      Concatenation (‖)
      Gluing lists end to end: [1, 2] ‖ [3] = [1, 2, 3]. The report's equations use the ‖ sign.
      Backpropagation
      How the network works out each knob's share of the blame for an error, passing the blame backwards from the output to the first layer, so gradient descent knows which knobs to turn.
      Embedding
      A short list of numbers that sums up something complicated, such as "this coin's situation right now", in a form the network can compare and combine.
      RNN, LSTM and GRU
      Networks that read a sequence one step at a time and keep a running memory. LSTM and GRU add gates: valves that decide what to keep, what to forget and what to overwrite.A GRU updates each coin's memory in the TGN; the LSTM baseline reads one coin's last week.
      Attention and heads
      Attention gives each input a weight, all the weights adding up to 1, so the important ones count more; the network learns the weights. Several heads do this side by side, each free to look for something different.The TGN has 2 heads looking at each coin's 10 latest events.
      Transformer
      A network built mostly from attention ("Attention is all you need", 2017), the basis of ChatGPT-style models.DyGFormer is a small Transformer.
      Graph: node and edge
      Dots and lines. Nodes are the things (coins); edges are relationships. Directed edges have arrows (who affects whom); weighted edges have strengths.
      GNN and message passing
      A graph neural network: each node updates itself by collecting "messages" from its neighbours. A GCN averages its neighbours; a GAT uses attention over them.
      Temporal graph and event stream
      A graph whose links happen at particular times. The data is a list of time-stamped events."Coin i shocked at hour t; coin j is the one we ask about."
      Memory (TGN)
      A 64-number diary for each coin, rewritten by the GRU every time the coin takes part in an event.
      Message (TGN)
      The bundle of numbers used to update a coin's diary: both coins' diaries, the event's 5 numbers and a time code.
      Time encoding
      Turning "how long since this coin was last updated" into 16 numbers made from waves (cosines), so the network can tell an hour from a day.
      Link prediction
      The usual job of TGN-style models: will these two nodes interact? We changed the question to "will coin j shock after coin i?".
      Focal loss
      A loss that pays less attention to easy, obvious examples and more to the rare, hard ones. γ = 2 sets how strongly easy cases are quietened; α = 0.25 sets the weight on "yes" cases.
      Dropout and weight decay
      Dropout switches off a random 20% of neurons at each training step so the network cannot lean on any one of them. Weight decay gently pulls every knob towards zero so none becomes extreme.
      Autoencoder
      A network trained to rebuild its own input through a narrow middle. Things it rebuilds badly are unusual: anomalies.The T-GAE, our failed stress detector.
      Attribution (explainability)
      Splitting a prediction into how much each input pushed it up or down.Integrated gradients and TempME.
      Adversarial attack, white-box, ε
      Changing inputs a tiny bit on purpose to fool a model. White-box means the attacker knows the model inside out; ε (epsilon) is the size of the change.FGSM and PGD.
      Quantisation, fp32, int8
      fp32 stores each knob as a precise 32-bit decimal; int8 as an 8-bit whole number: four times less space, but rounded.
      CPU and GPU
      A CPU is a computer's normal processor; a GPU is a graphics chip that does many multiplications at once, usually faster for deep learning.All our final runs used a CPU; no GPU was needed.
      PyTorch, PyTorch Geometric, scikit-learn, Streamlit
      The software libraries: PyTorch for neural networks, PyTorch Geometric (PyG) for graph models, scikit-learn for classic machine learning such as boosting, and Streamlit to turn Python into a web page (the dashboard).
      Checkpoint, commit, run registry
      A checkpoint is a saved copy of a trained model's knobs. A git commit is a saved snapshot of the code. The run registry logs every training run with its commit, so any number can be traced back.

      If a word in a question is unclear, it is fine to restate it: "By volatility you mean how jumpy the price is; we measure it as the standard deviation of recent hourly returns." Restating shows understanding and buys a few seconds to think.

      Part 1

      The whole project in one minute

      Three versions of the same story, from a child to the professor.

      Simple version

      Think of 30 cryptocurrencies as 30 kids in a classroom. Every so often one kid suddenly does something wild. Often, within a few hours, other kids start doing wild things too, the way a yawn spreads around a room.

      Our program watches all 30 coins hour by hour. When one coin makes a sudden big move, it guesses which of the other coins will make a big move of their own in the next 1, 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours, and how likely that is. It explains its guesses in plain words and shows them on a website, together with how it did in past crises.

      For a friend

      We built a system that predicts shock contagion in crypto: when one coin has an unusually big hourly price move, which other coins will have one soon after. We used six and a half years of hourly prices for 30 coins from Binance, turned every big move into a set of "will this coin be next?" questions, and trained a Temporal Graph Network (TGN) to answer them. We compared it with simpler models, explained what drives its answers, tested how easily it breaks, and built a plain-language dashboard that shows its latest predictions and replays past crises.

      For the professor: the 60-second pitch

      Say it in the viva
      "CryptoGraph-Secure predicts hourly shock contagion between cryptocurrencies. We built a leakage-controlled dataset of 30 coins from Binance, January 2020 to September 2026, with fixed date splits and a 7-day embargo. Whenever a coin has an abnormal move, we create an event towards every other coin, labelled by whether that coin also shocks within 1 to 24 hours: 1.46 million events. Our main model is a Temporal Graph Network, which keeps a memory for each coin and reads events strictly in time order. On the 2024 to 2026 test period it reaches an AUPRC of 0.205 at one hour, about twice the random level of 0.105, and beats a fitted independent-cascade model at every horizon, confirmed with a day-block bootstrap. Its riskiest 1% of alerts come true three to five times as often as random picks. The honest finding is that the signal lives mostly in the receiving coin's own state, and the graph memory adds value mainly at 6 to 24 hours. We also built explanations, robustness tests and a plain-language Streamlit dashboard."

      The full title in the report is "CryptoGraph-Secure: Predicting Cryptocurrency Shock Contagion with Temporal Graph Networks". The work was done from late September to October 2026 by Hardik Gulati and Dakshesh Chauhan (AIML-A, SIT Pune).

      Part 2

      The problem: shocks and how they spread

      After this part you can say exactly what the project predicts, and why it is useful and hard.

      A few words first

      • Cryptocurrency (coin): digital money such as Bitcoin (BTC) or Ethereum (ETH). Its price changes all day, every day, with no closing time.
      • Exchange: a marketplace where coins are bought and sold. We use Binance, one of the largest.
      • USDT (Tether): a coin designed to stay worth one US dollar. On Binance, prices are quoted in USDT, so "BTC at 80,000" means 80,000 USDT.
      • Hourly bar (candle): a summary of one hour of trading: the opening price, highest, lowest, closing price and how much was traded (volume).

      What is a shock?

      Simple version

      Every coin has its own normal amount of wobble. Bitcoin might normally move 0.5% in an hour, a meme coin 2%. A shock is an hour when a coin moves much more than its own normal wobble: about 2.3 times its usual hourly move or more, up or down. It is like a usually calm kid suddenly shouting.

      "Normal" is measured from the coin's own previous week (168 hours), so a jumpy coin needs a bigger move to count. Both crashes and sudden jumps count, because both are risky. Shocks happen in about 4% of hours.

      What is contagion?

      Contagion means a shock spreading from one coin to others, like a sneeze going round a class or aftershocks after an earthquake. In crypto this happens fast: when the Terra/LUNA coin collapsed in May 2022 or the FTX exchange failed in November 2022, most big coins dropped within hours. On 10 October 2025 a tariff headline set off about 19 billion dollars of forced selling in roughly a day.

      The exact question we answer

      Say it in the viva

      "Coin i has a shock at hour t. For every other coin j, we predict whether j will have a shock of its own within the next k hours, for k = 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24. Only strictly later hours count: if j moves in the same hour, that is moving together, not spreading." This is task T1, the main task.

      Two side tasks were also built, mainly as baselines:

      • T2: will a given coin have a shock in the next hour? (an LSTM model)
      • T3: how much will a coin's price wobble over the next 1, 6 and 24 hours (future volatility)? (an LSTM against a classic formula called HAR-RV)

      We never predict whether a price goes up or down. Our early analysis showed hourly direction is close to unpredictable (returns barely relate to past returns), while the size of moves clearly clusters in time (volatility clustering). So we predict the part that has signal.

      Why it matters

      • Traders and funds: cut risk on the coins most likely to be hit next.
      • Exchanges: raise safety margins before a wave of forced selling.
      • Regulators and researchers: understand how linked the market is (systemic risk).

      Classic finance research measures how strongly markets are linked on average (correlation, spillover indices). It does not say who will be hit next, and how soon. That gap is our contribution.

      Why it is hard

      • Rare: after a shock, only about 10% of other coins shock in the next hour.
      • All at once: most big moves are news-driven and hit many coins in the same hour, which leaves little "first this coin, then that one" order to learn.
      • Noisy: crypto prices are jumpy, and the market changes over the years.
      • Easy to cheat by accident: any information from the future that slips into the inputs makes results look great but impossible in real life (data leakage).
      Careful

      Why "Secure" in the name? The original plan stressed security and robustness: attacks on the model, anomaly detection and trustworthy data. In the final project this means the robustness tests (noise, missing data, deliberate attacks), the anomaly-detection experiment, checksum-verified data and safe model loading. If asked, say that, and do not claim it detects hacks or fraud.

      Part 3

      The data: what we collected and how we cleaned it

      After this part you can say where every number came from and why the data can be trusted.

      Where it comes from

      Prices come from the Binance public data archive (data.binance.vision): free, official files of hourly bars. Each file comes with a SHA-256 checksum, a digital fingerprint. We checked every downloaded file against its fingerprint: 3,049 files, zero failures. If a single byte had been corrupted, the fingerprint would not match.

      The period runs from 1 January 2020 to 27 September 2026, every hour, in UTC time: 59,088 hours for each coin, about 1.6 million coin-hours in total. A separate daily price dataset (Yahoo Finance and Coin Metrics) was used only to double-check the hourly prices; they agreed to about 0.1% on a typical day.

      Why hourly, not daily?

      Simple version

      If you only look at the class once a day, you see that ten kids were wild today, but not who started it. Looking every hour shows the order.

      • traded against USDT on Binance;
      • at least 2 years (730 days) of hourly history;
      • data for at least 90% of the test-period hours.

      The golden rule: no peeking at the future

      Simple version

      Studying with last year's exam answers taped inside your pencil case gives a great score that means nothing. Our model must never see anything from the future, even by accident.

      • Everything the model sees at hour t uses only data up to hour t.
      • Every setting fitted from data (scaling, time-of-day pattern, the shock threshold, the network threshold) is fitted on the training years only.
      • The answer for hour t only looks at later hours.
      • Automated tests check these rules, for example that changing data after t never changes anything computed at t.

      Splitting time into practice, mock exam and final exam

      Simple version

      • A 7-day embargo (gap) at the start of validation and test, and no example whose 24-hour answer window crosses into the next period.

      Part 4

      Turning prices into a question a computer can answer

      After this part you can explain a shock, an event and a label with a worked example.

      Step 1: spot the shocks

      For each coin and hour we compute a z-score: this hour's move divided by the coin's normal hourly move over the previous 168 hours (not counting this hour, so no peeking).

      z = (this hour's return, time-of-day adjusted) ÷ (typical hourly move over the previous week)

      If |z| is above 2.29, the hour is a shock. Why 2.29? It is the level at which about 4% of training hours count as shocks, matching the 3.9% rate of the earlier daily data. It was set on training data only, so the test period could not influence it.

      Step 2: turn each shock into events

      When coin i shocks at hour t, we create one event for every other coin j that is trading at that hour: "i just shocked; what about j?" With 30 coins, one shock makes up to 29 events. If five coins shock in the same hour, that is up to 5 × 29 = 145 events.

      Each event carries five numbers, called the message:

      1. how often j followed i one hour later over the past week (lag-1 correlation);
      2. how closely i and j move in the same hour (same-hour correlation);
      3. the size of i's move now;
      4. how jumpy i is now;
      5. a "this is a shock" flag. Every event is a shock, so this one is always 1 and carries no information. It is a harmless leftover; mention it if asked about weaknesses.

      Step 3: write down the answers (labels)

      For every event we record five yes/no answers: does j shock within the next 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 hours, counting only hours t+1 onwards? One event, five labels. The model gives five probabilities at once.

      Numbers to know
      • 1.46 million events in total: 360,590 train, 300,998 validation, 801,559 test.
      • Share of "yes" answers on the test period (the positive rate): 10.5% within 1 hour, 20.6% within 3, 31.0% within 6, 44.7% within 12, 62.7% within 24.
      • Each event: 5 message numbers; each coin: 14 feature numbers.
      Careful

      The answer for coin j depends only on j and the hour, not on which coin triggered the event. If BTC and ETH both shock at 14:00, SOL gets two events with the same five answers. So in practice the model answers "after a shock somewhere, which coins move next?". This is why the dashboard shows one chance per coin (the highest over the events pointing at it), and why the explanations find that the receiving coin's own state matters most. If asked, say it plainly: it is a known property of the label, and a direction-aware label ("does j crash after i crashes?") is listed as future work.

      Part 5

      How we judge a model

      After this part you can explain AUPRC, why random scores 0.105, and why we resample whole days.

      The accuracy trap

      Simple version

      Imagine a weather app in a desert that always says "no rain". It is right 95% of the time and completely useless. Accuracy is a bad score when the thing you care about is rare.

      Within 1 hour, 89.5% of our events are "no". A lazy model that always says "no" would be 89.5% accurate and would never warn anyone. So we never report accuracy.

      Precision and recall

      Think of a smoke alarm.

      • Precision: of the times it beeped, how often was there really a fire? If we flag 10 coins and 4 of them move, precision is 4 out of 10 = 40%.
      • Recall: of all the real fires, how many did it catch? If 20 coins moved and we flagged 4 of them, recall is 4 out of 20 = 20%.

      Flagging more coins raises recall but usually lowers precision. Every model has to trade one for the other.

      AUPRC: one score for the whole trade-off

      Our model does not just say yes or no; it gives each event a probability. Sort all events from most to least risky. Walk down the list. Every time you reach a coin that really moved, write down your precision so far. The average of those numbers is the average precision, also called AUPRC (area under the precision-recall curve).

      • A perfect model puts every real move at the top of the list: AUPRC = 1.0.
      • Random guessing scores the positive rate: 0.105 within 1 hour, because a random list is right 10.5% of the time at every depth.
      • So the TGN's 0.205 at 1 hour means it is about twice as good as random. That ratio is the lift.
      Careful

      Never compare AUPRC across horizons directly. Within 24 hours, 62.7% of coins move anyway, so random already scores 0.627, and our 0.739 is a smaller lift (about 1.2×) than 0.205 is at 1 hour (about 2×). Always quote AUPRC next to the random level.

      We also report AUROC: the chance that a randomly picked coin that moved was ranked above a randomly picked coin that did not. 0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect. The TGN gets 0.64 to 0.69: clearly better than random, but modest. Most shocks are news-driven and arrive across the whole market at once, which caps how predictable they are.

      Two scores people understand instantly

      • Top-1% precision: of the 1% of alerts the model is most sure about, how many came true? For the TGN on the test period: 32% within 1 hour (random 10.5%), 61% within 3 hours (random 21%), 76% within 6 hours (random 31%) and 89% within 24 hours (random 63%).
      • Calibration: when the model says 30%, does it happen about 30% of the time? Raw model scores are not true chances, so we convert them with isotonic regression (an always-rising curve) fitted on the validation years. The report lists checking this on the test years as still open; our final review did check it, and the chances the dashboard shows matched what happened within a few percentage points.

      Making comparisons fair

      1. Five seeds. A neural network starts from random knob settings, so two trainings give slightly different results. We train every final neural model five times (seeds 0 to 4) and report the average. The spread between seeds is about ±0.005 AUPRC, so smaller differences mean nothing.
      2. Same events for everyone. Every model is scored on exactly the same test events with the same answers.
      3. A day-block bootstrap. To ask "is model A really better than B, or was it luck?", we rebuild the test period 1,000 times by drawing its 971 days at random with replacement, and compute A's score minus B's score each time. The middle 95% of those 1,000 differences is the 95% interval. If the whole interval is above zero, A is reliably better.
      4. Why whole days? One crash creates hundreds of events that are all related. Resampling single events would pretend they are independent and make us overconfident. Resampling days keeps each day's events together.
      5. Test once. All tuning used validation. Each final model scored the test period once, and every version that ever touched the test period is listed in the report (Table VI), including the ones we did not adopt.
      Say it in the viva

      "The task is imbalanced, about 10% positives at one hour, so accuracy is meaningless. We use average precision, whose random baseline equals the positive rate, plus AUROC and the precision of the top 1% of alerts. Differences between models are tested with a paired day-block bootstrap over 971 test days, because events within a day are strongly dependent."

      Part 6

      The building blocks, from scratch

      Everything you need to understand the TGN: parameters, training, graphs, memory and attention.

      A neural network is a recipe with adjustable knobs

      Simple version

      Imagine a huge mixing desk with thousands of knobs. Numbers go in one side (a coin's recent moves), pass through the desk, and one number comes out (the chance of a shock). At first the knobs are random and the answers are nonsense. Training means showing the desk thousands of past examples and, after each one, turning every knob a tiny bit in the direction that would have made the answer less wrong.

      • Parameters (weights): the knobs. Our TGN has 70,021 of them. Each is a single number the training adjusts.
      • Layer: one stage of the desk. A simple layer multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up, and passes the total through a small bend (ReLU: keep it if positive, else zero), which lets the network learn curved, not just straight-line, patterns.
      • Sigmoid: the last step squeezes any number into a value between 0 and 1, read as a probability. Our model has five outputs, one per time window, each with its own sigmoid.

      How training works

      1. Loss: a number that measures how wrong the answers are. Lower is better.
      2. Gradient: for every knob, which way to turn it to lower the loss. Computed automatically (backpropagation).
      3. Optimizer (Adam): the rule for how far to turn each knob. Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) adapts the step size for every knob on its own. Our learning rate, the overall step size, is 0.0005.
      4. Batch: we learn from about 200 events at a time, not one by one.
      5. Epoch: one full pass over all training events. The TGN trains for up to 100 epochs.
      6. Early stopping: after every epoch we check the validation score. If it has not improved for 10 epochs in a row (the patience), we stop and keep the best version.

      Overfitting is memorising the practice papers instead of learning the subject: great on training data, poor on new data. Three guards against it:

      • Dropout: during training, 20% of the head's neurons are switched off at random, so no single neuron becomes a crutch.
      • Weight decay: a small pull of every knob back towards zero (0.0001), which keeps the model simple.
      • Early stopping on validation, and a deliberately small model.

      Focal loss: paying attention to the hard cases

      Because "no" is nine times more common than "yes", a normal loss lets the model coast on easy "no" answers. Focal loss (Lin and colleagues at Facebook AI, 2017) turns down the weight of examples the model already gets right and turns up the weight of hard, rare ones. Settings: focusing strength γ = 2, positive weight α = 0.25. It was chosen over a weighted ordinary loss by validation AUPRC.

      Graphs: dots and lines

      Simple version

      A graph is a drawing of dots joined by lines. In a friendship graph, the dots are people and the lines are friendships. In ours, the dots (nodes) are the 30 coins and the lines (edges) are relationships: two coins that move together, or one coin's shock pointing at another coin.

      A graph neural network (GNN) lets each dot update what it knows by listening to its neighbours, a step called message passing. A coin linked to many jittery coins learns that it lives in a jittery neighbourhood.

      Temporal graphs: when the lines appear matters

      In our data, lines appear at particular moments: "BTC shocked at 14:00, pointing at SOL". The order matters (a shock at 13:00 can explain one at 15:00, never the reverse). A graph whose lines carry timestamps is a temporal (or dynamic) graph. Ours is a stream of timed events, a continuous-time dynamic graph. Older methods instead take a snapshot of the graph once a day, which loses the order of events inside the day.

      Memory: a diary for every coin (the GRU)

      Simple version

      Each coin keeps a little diary, 64 numbers long. Every time something happens to the coin, the diary is updated: some old notes are kept, some are overwritten. The diary lets the model remember "this coin has been nervous for the last two days" without re-reading all of history.

      The diary is updated by a GRU (gated recurrent unit; Cho and colleagues, 2014). It has two "gates", small learned switches that decide how much of the old memory to keep and how much new information to let in. Its cousin, the LSTM, does the same job with three gates.

      Attention: deciding what to focus on

      Simple version

      When you read a sentence you do not give every word equal weight. Attention does the same for the model: it looks at a coin's 10 most recent events and gives each a weight (all weights add up to 1), so the important ones count more.

      Technically, the coin asks a query ("what am I looking for?"), each past event offers a key ("what I'm about") and a value ("what I'd contribute"); the weights come from how well the query matches each key. This is the idea behind the Transformer (2017). Our TGN uses two attention "heads" in parallel, each able to focus on something different.

      Time encoding and embeddings

      • Time encoding: a model cannot use "3 hours ago" well as a single raw number, so the time gap is turned into 16 numbers built from waves of different speeds (cosines), which let it tell very recent events from old ones.
      • Embedding: a short list of numbers that summarises something complicated. The TGN turns each coin's memory plus its recent events into a 64-number embedding.
      Part 7

      The main model: the Temporal Graph Network (TGN)

      After this part you can say what it is, who made it and when, how it works box by box, how many parameters it has and why we chose it.

      Simple version

      The TGN keeps a diary for every coin. When a coin has a big move, the TGN reads the diaries of that coin and of each other coin, glances at what happened to each of them recently, looks at how both are behaving right now, and gives five chances: will the other coin have a big move within 1, 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours? Only after it has answered for the whole hour does it write the hour's events into the diaries. It can never use the future to answer.

      Who made it, and when

      The TGN was introduced in the paper "Temporal Graph Networks for Deep Learning on Dynamic Graphs" by Emanuele Rossi, Ben Chamberlain, Fabrizio Frasca, Davide Eynard, Federico Monti and Michael Bronstein, researchers at Twitter working with Imperial College London. It was first published in June 2020 (arXiv 2006.10637) and presented at an ICML 2020 workshop. It was designed for streams of interactions such as users replying on Twitter, editing Wikipedia or posting on Reddit, and it became a standard model for this kind of data.

      It combines older ideas: memory updated by a GRU (2014), attention over recent neighbours as in TGAT (Xu and colleagues, Walmart Labs, 2020) and the Transformer (2017), and learnable time encoding.

      Our version, called ContagionTGN in src/cryptograph/models/tgn.py, is built with PyTorch Geometric (PyG), a widely used graph library for PyTorch (Fey and Lenssen, 2019). We use PyG's memory module (TGNMemory), its recent-neighbour store (LastNeighborLoader) and its graph-transformer attention layer (TransformerConv), and we wrote our own prediction head for the five time windows and our own training loop (src/cryptograph/training/tgn_task.py).

      Step by step

      1. An event arrives: coin i shocked at hour t; the event points at coin j and carries its 5-number message.
      2. Read the diaries: fetch the 64-number memories of i and j.
      3. Look at recent events: for each of the two coins, fetch its 10 most recent events, with the time since each one, turned into a 16-number time code.
      4. Attention: a graph-transformer layer with 2 heads weighs those 10 events and turns each coin's memory plus its recent events into a 64-number embedding.
      5. Join everything: both embeddings (64 + 64), both coins' current 14 features (14 + 14) and the event's 5 numbers: 161 numbers in total. A small two-layer network (the head) turns them into 64 numbers and then into 5.
      6. Five chances: a sigmoid turns each of the 5 outputs into a probability, one per time window. Each window gets its own probability (not a softmax), because "within 6 hours" includes "within 3 hours".
      7. Update the diaries: only after every event of hour t has been scored, both coins' memories are updated with the event by the GRU. Events are processed in time order in batches of about 200, and a batch never splits an hour, so events in the same hour cannot see each other.

      How it was trained

      • Each epoch replays the training events in time order with memory starting from zero; validation continues straight after, so memory carries over in time, as it would in real life.
      • For the final scores, the model replays training (without learning), then validation, then test, so memory is warmed up exactly as it would be in real use.
      • On a laptop CPU (no GPU), each of the five seeds took about 30 to 50 minutes; the best epochs were between 45 and 98. One pass over the full 1.46 million events takes one to two minutes.
      • The first final version (up to 30 epochs, patience 4) stopped while its validation curve was still rising (0.138). Looking only at validation curves, we raised the budget to 100 epochs with patience 10 (version 2, validation 0.153), and that became the final model.
      • Variants tried on validation and not adopted: a learning-rate schedule (0.151), extra context features (0.151), stronger regularisation (0.143), a smaller 32-number model (0.133) and futures-market data (0.131). None beat version 2 by more than the seed-to-seed noise (±0.005), so we kept the simplest well-trained model.

      Why we chose the TGN

      1. The data is a stream of timed events. Shocks happen at specific hours and point from one coin to others. The TGN was built exactly for such continuous-time event streams; most other graph models expect fixed snapshots.
      2. Memory fits the problem. A coin that has been nervous for two days is more likely to move again. The memory tracks that without re-reading history, and our test (memory on against off) shows it helps for the 6 to 24 hour windows.
      3. It uses both sides of the story: each coin's own state (like an LSTM) and its relationships with other coins (like a graph model).
      4. It is honest about time. Predict first, update memory after, which matches our no-peeking rule naturally.
      5. It is small and fast: 70 thousand parameters, CPU training, a full prediction in under a minute.
      6. It is well established as a standard model for dynamic graphs, with a maintained implementation in PyTorch Geometric, so we were not building on untested code.
      7. It can be explained: TempME, a recent explainer for temporal graph models, works on TGN-style models, and we needed explanations.

      Why not the alternatives?

      • An LSTM alone reads one coin's history and ignores the other coins. We used it as a baseline for the single-coin tasks.
      • A static graph network (GCN or GAT on one graph) ignores time entirely.
      • A snapshot graph network (one graph per day, plus a GRU over the days) loses the order of events inside each day. It was planned and dropped, because the TGN, DyGFormer and boosting comparison already answers whether the graph matters.
      • A big Transformer on raw price series needs far more data than 30 coins provide. DyGFormer is the Transformer-style dynamic-graph model, and we did test it as a second opinion.
      • The official DyGLib library trains with random fake links and splits time by fractions, which would break our leakage rules, so we re-implemented DyGFormer compactly instead.
      Careful

      The TGN is not a price predictor (it never says up or down), not a trading bot, and not a language model. And memory does not help within 1 hour: the version without memory scores the same there (0.208 against 0.205). Say this before the professor finds it: memory pays off for the slower, multi-hour spread.

      The five findings in plain words

      1. Every model beats random. There is real, if modest, signal. The TGN is about twice as good as random within 1 hour.
      2. The TGN clearly beats the network-science model. Against the independent cascade it is better at every window: +0.046 at 1 hour (95% interval 0.019 to 0.074) and +0.084 at 6 hours (0.051 to 0.116). In none of the 1,000 bootstrap rebuilds did the cascade come out ahead.

      What drives an alert (explanations)

      Integrated gradients measure how much each input pushed a score up or down compared with an average situation; the pushes add up exactly to the score (we check this "completeness" every run). Averaged over alerts:

      • about 65% comes from the receiving coin's own state: its volume compared with its past week (25%), its jumpiness over the last day (12%) and its last move (9%);
      • about 25% from the coin that shocked;
      • about 10% from the event itself; memory and recent events about 7%.
      Simple version

      The model mostly flags coins that are already stretched (unusually busy and jumpy) at the moment another coin crashes. That is the same pattern in the LUNA, FTX and October 2025 crashes.

      TempME looks for small chains of past events ("motifs") that explain an alert. We ported it to our TGN. It turned out barely better than picking events at random (a fidelity score of 0.157 against 0.152), worse than simply taking the most recent events (0.192), and far from the best possible choice (0.248). The reason: the TGN's attention over recent events is almost perfectly even, and shocks spread across the market within the same hour rather than along chains, so there are no chains to find. That is a finding about the market, not a bug.

      What we learned about the market

      • One common factor (the whole market moving together) explains 64% of hourly price movement.
      • "Coin A moves, then coin B an hour later" is very weak (a typical lagged correlation of 0.035). Coins move together within the hour, which is why the graph has no fixed "who leads whom" lines.
      • Groups of coins that move together partly match their sectors, but contagion ignores sectors: after a shock, another coin's chance of shocking is about the same inside and outside the sector. Meme coins are the exception.
      • In all nine catalogued crises, correlation, spillover and shock spreading rise and the network's skeleton (MST) shrinks.

      How long shocks last (notebook 05)

      • Across 18,171 "fresh" shocks (no shock of the same coin in the previous day), a coin's jumpiness was back to normal after a median of 8 to 10 hours (28% within 6 hours, 87% within 24, 99% within 72).
      • 55 to 65% of shocks are followed by another shock of the same coin within 24 hours.
      • After a market-wide crash about 8 other coins shock within 6 hours, against 4.5 to 5 after an isolated shock.
      • The extra jumpiness compared with a matched calm hour halves in about 2.5 hours in every sector.

      How easily it breaks (robustness, notebook 06)

      • Removing up to half of the past events: the score changes by under 0.5%. Missing data is not a problem.
      • Random noise of half a standard deviation on every input: 5.7% lower (boosting: 4.7%).
      • A deliberate attack (FGSM, Goodfellow and colleagues, 2015), which nudges inputs in exactly the direction that hurts the model most, by only 0.1 standard deviations: about 35% lower. Random noise of the same size costs under 1%. A stronger 10-step attack (PGD) was barely worse than FGSM.
      • The explanations are stable: under small input noise, the top reason for an alert stays the same for 97 to 99% of alerts.
      Say it in the viva

      "The model is robust to missing and noisy data but vulnerable to targeted manipulation: a gradient attack of 0.1 standard deviations cuts one-hour AUPRC by about 35%, while random noise of the same size costs under 1%. That is the 'secure' part: it tells you what a deployed system must defend against, such as multi-source input checks or adversarial training."

      Making it run cheaply (compression)

      We tried shrinking the TGN to 8-bit numbers (int8 quantisation): 3.1 times smaller (94 kB against 296 kB) but 1.8 times slower on CPU, because the time goes into looking up recent events and updating memory, not into the arithmetic, and it cost 0.004 to 0.007 AUPRC. So the dashboard serves the normal full-size model: about 6 microseconds per event.

      A check from the final code review: a much simpler model

      Careful

      In our last review we also tried a logistic regression (one weighted sum, no deep learning) on just four numbers: the receiving coin's volume against its past week, hours since its last shock, how much of the market shocked in the last day, and how big its current move is.       If asked "did you compare with a simple model?", answer honestly: "Yes. The independent cascade and boosting are in the report, and in our final review a four-feature logistic regression matched the TGN. It confirms what our explanations show: at hourly resolution, a coin's own turbulence and the market's breadth carry most of the signal, because shocks spread across the market within the same hour. The TGN learns this automatically from the raw event stream, its memory captures what 'hours since the last shock' measures, and it clearly beats the network-diffusion baseline. Finer data, such as order books or minute bars, is where a graph model could pull ahead."

        Stationarity (ADF test). Log prices have no stable level for 15 of 20 coins; returns are stable for all 20. So every input is built from returns and volatility, never from prices.
      5. 5
        Fat tails (kurtosis and Jarque–Bera). Median excess kurtosis 18 (a bell curve has 0); the bell curve is rejected for all 20 coins; Bitcoin's worst day was −37% (12 March 2020). So: Huber loss for volatility, inputs clipped at ±5 after scaling, and scores built for rare events.
      6. 6
        Direction vs volatility (autocorrelation). Yesterday's direction says nothing about today's (lag-1 correlation −0.024; the same sign only 47% of days), but calm follows calm and storms follow storms (+0.184 for squared returns). So the project predicts shocks and volatility, not up or down.
      7. 7
        Shock and contagion labels, daily version. A shock is |z| > 2.5 against the previous 30 days' volatility, measured up to the day before; contagion is coin j shocking within 1 to 3 days after coin i. Shocks are about 4% of days. For a random pair of coins a follow-on shock is rare (under 1%), but right after coin i shocks, coin j shocks within 1 to 3 days 6 to 14% of the time. That jump is the signal the graph model must learn.
      8. 8
        Scaling on training data only (robust scaling). Each feature is centred on the coin's median and divided by its interquartile range, fitted on training dates only. BTC's and HBAR's raw volumes sit at completely different levels; after scaling they look alike, so the model sees "unusually busy for this coin" and nothing from the future leaks in.
      9. 9
        Splits, embargo and a calmer future. Fixed dates chosen so each period contains a big crash: training to June 2022 (2018 crash, COVID, Terra), validation to December 2023 (FTX), test from 2024 (the October 2025 liquidations), with a 30-day embargo on daily data (7 days on hourly). Training was far more volatile (107% a year against 63% and 72%), yet shocks stay at about 4% of days in every period, because each move is judged against the coin's own recent volatility.
      10. 10
        Correlation: why a graph model. Every pair of coins moves together to some degree (correlation 0.39 to 0.80, average 0.57), and more so after crises (0.61 in the 30 days after, against 0.54). A one-coin model cannot use this shared movement; a graph model can.
      11. 11
        The daily graph and its threshold. Two coins are linked when their 30-day correlation is at least 0.6, plus each coin's two strongest links. Lower thresholds (0.3 to 0.4) link almost every pair, which carries no information. "Coin i yesterday, coin j today" links appear no more often than chance (1.6 to 4.3% against 5.4%), so the TGN gets direction from which coin shocked first, not from fixed arrows.

      Notebook 04 · Why does the TGN raise an alert?

      04_tempme_explanations.ipynb · 25 cells · explains the final TGN, seed 0

      Two levels of "why": which past events drive an alert (TempME), and which inputs drive it (integrated gradients). The explainer was trained on validation events; everything reported is on the test period (the riskiest 1% of alerts at 6 hours plus a random sample) and on three crashes.

      1. 1
        Reference rankings. The coin rankings from notebook 02 that explanations are compared with. The correlation-based ones are one and the same ranking; the time-ordered shock PageRank is related but different (about 0.73). Computed on the test window and never used as model inputs.
      2. 2
        How the explanations are made, and two exactness checks. For each event the TGN looks at the 10 most recent events of each coin (up to 20). Removing an event means giving it zero weight in the attention; the memory is left as it is. The captured inputs reproduce the TGN's own outputs to 2.4 × 10−7 (rebuild check), and the integrated-gradient pushes add up to the output difference within 0.009. TempME's explainer was trained on 10,000 validation events with the official settings; its score flattened after 3 epochs and training stopped at epoch 13: it found very little to learn.
      3. 3
        Do the chosen events carry the prediction? (fidelity and characterization). With 3 events kept: TempME 0.157 on top alerts, random choice 0.152, the TGN's own attention 0.147, the 3 most recent events 0.192, and a mask tuned separately for every alert, the best any event-level explanation can do, 0.248. Removing all recent events moves a prediction by only about 0.013 in probability. The influence of past events is spread almost evenly; no small set explains an alert.
      4. 4
        Why event explanations are weak here. The TGN's attention is perfectly even (normalised entropy 1.000; the largest of 10 weights averages 0.100). Event patterns around real alerts look like those in a time-shuffled stream: 83% against 86% are three-step walks through four different coins; the only excess is walks that come back to a coin (5.9% against 3.3%). Shocks spread across the market within the hour, not along chains, so there are no special chains for TempME to find.
      5. 5
        What drives an alert (integrated gradients, 6-hour window, baseline = the training average). The receiving coin about 65%, the shocking coin 25%, the event itself 10%. Biggest single driver: the receiver's volume against its past week (25%), then its 24-hour volatility (12%) and its latest return (9%). Memory and recent events together about 7%. The split is the same for top alerts, random events and all three crashes: one consistent rule, "a coin already trading heavily and moving a lot is the one most likely to be hit next".
      6. 6
        Do explanations point at the central coins? (plausibility). TempME's credit runs the other way: about −0.8 against the spillover and PageRank rankings, −0.65 against shock PageRank. It credits loners (TRX, WLD, ZEC, BCH, QNT) most and BTC and ETH least. The tuned mask points the same way but weaker (−0.2 to −0.4); the TGN's attention follows how often a coin appears (+0.3). Because TempME is barely better than random here, its credit is not evidence of who causes contagion; notebook 02's rankings are the better measure.
      7. 7
        Three crashes. The five riskiest 6-hour alerts in each: LUNA (12 May 2022, training period) 4 of 5 came true (chance about 0.57); FTX (8 to 9 November 2022, validation) 4 of 5 (about 0.59); 10 October 2025 (test) 5 of 5 (about 0.65). 13 of 15 in all. The leading reasons are always the receiver's 24-hour volatility and volume surge, and TempME's top events are those of the previous 1 to 3 hours involving the same coins (for example AVAX to WLD at 20:00 before the 21:00 WLD alerts).

      Notebook 05 · How long does a shock last?

      05_persistence_by_shock_type.ipynb · 19 cells · hourly data, 30 coins, 2020 to 2026

      Definitions first. A shock is the project's shock (|z| > 2.29). Only fresh shocks count (no shock of the same coin in the previous 24 hours), so one stormy episode counts once. Each shock gets a type by breadth, the share of other coins shocking in the same hour: market-wide 30% or more, partial 10 to 30%, idiosyncratic (a coin on its own) under 10%; and a direction, crash or pump. Intervals resample whole days, 500 times.

      1. 1
        Crises hour by hour (event windows). The middle coin's path from 24 hours before to 72 hours after each crisis date (cumulative log returns): COVID −53% by hour 24, the China ban −52%, FTX −15%, the yen carry-trade unwind −16% already by hour 6, the bitcoin ETF approval +9% by hour 24. Hour 0 is midnight UTC of the date, so some moves start before it.
      2. 2
        Hours back to normal (persistence). 18,171 fresh shocks out of 57,792 shock hours. A coin's volatility is back to its level before the shock after a median of 8 to 10 hours for every type (isolated crashes fastest, 8 hours; isolated pumps and market-wide crashes 10 hours); 28% within 6 hours, 87% within 24, 99% within 72. Average |z|: about 0.62 in the day before (calmer than the 0.72 average hour), 3.5 at a market-wide shock (about 3.0 for others), 1.1 an hour later, 0.8 after 6 hours. The same in training, validation and test (9 to 10 hours): a stable fact about this market.
      3. 3
        By type (re-shocks, followers, continuation). Other coins shocking within 6 hours: 8.1 after a market-wide crash, 7.2 after a market-wide pump, 6.2 after a partial shock, 4.5 to 5.1 after an isolated one. 55 to 65% of shocks are followed by another shock of the same coin within a day (isolated crashes least, 55%). Direction is mostly unpredictable; only pumps tend to keep going for a while (isolated pumps +0.34 standard deviations over 24 hours, market-wide pumps +0.42 over 6 hours).
      4. 4
        What it means for the models. Test events split by the type of the triggering shock: after market-wide and partial shocks the TGN beats boosting at every window (market-wide crash 0.245 against 0.205 at 1 hour; partial crash 0.130 against 0.103); after isolated shocks boosting is level or slightly ahead (0.117 against 0.114). Contagion is far more likely after a market-wide crash (16% at 1 hour against 4 to 6% after isolated shocks). Three consequences: alerts matter most in the first 6 to 24 hours; a real system could use the TGN after broad shocks and a simpler model after isolated ones; no separate persistence model is needed, because the 1 to 24-hour labels already cover a shock's whole life.
      5. 5
        Against an ordinary hour, and each crisis's reach (matched null, blast radius). Comparing the 72 hours after each fresh shock with those after a random calm hour of the same coin in the same month (17,591 shocks), the extra jumpiness halves in about 2.5 hours in every sector (2.35 to 2.56), with no differences between sectors and a small tail still visible at 72 hours. Every catalogued crisis was market-wide at its busiest hour (15 to 29 coins at once) and reached every active coin within 72 hours. They differ in strength: LUNA raised the shock rate most (+0.19 against the week before), then COVID (+0.10) and FTX (+0.07); the bitcoin ETF approval barely (+0.01). Peak spillover came 1 hour after the start for FTX and 21 hours for Celsius.
      Simple version

      A shock is like a stone thrown in a pond. The big splash is over in two or three hours, the ripples are gone in about half a day, and a big stone (a market-wide crash) rocks about twice as many boats as a pebble.

      Notebook 06 · Can it be fooled, and can it spot stress?

      06_robustness_tgae.ipynb · 17 cells · the "secure" part of CryptoGraph-Secure

      Two questions. Can a temporal graph autoencoder (T-GAE) flag unusual market days? And how stable are the contagion models when their inputs are noisy, missing or deliberately manipulated? A stress day, the yardstick, has at least one hour in which 70% or more of active coins shocked (20.7% of days); it comes from the returns, not from the T-GAE. The stress tests use the final TGN (seed 0) and the final boosting model on the test events, after a clean replay reproduces their saved predictions (largest difference 0.0002).

      1. 1
        The T-GAE against stress days. Its score for same-day stress reaches AUROC 0.511 on test (0.557 on validation), close to a coin flip, while the day's plain turbulence (average |z|) reaches 0.893. As a next-day warning: 0.490 against 0.600. Adding the T-GAE to turbulence changes almost nothing (0.893 to 0.898; next day 0.600 to 0.589). The five seeds agree within 0.01, so this is not bad luck. Why: an autoencoder learns to reproduce even extreme days, so its error measures unusual structure, not turbulence. Two designs were tried on validation; neither found a signal.
      2. 2
        Noise and missing events (noise, missing events). With 10, 25 or even 50% of test events never reaching the TGN's memory, its score moves by less than 0.5%: predictions rest on the coins' current state. Noise of half a standard deviation costs the TGN 5.7% and boosting 4.7% at 1 hour; a full standard deviation costs 16% against 8%, yet the TGN still scores higher at 1 hour (0.171 against 0.164). The TGN's probabilities move about half as much under the same noise (0.026 against 0.065).
      3. 3
        Deliberate attacks (FGSM, transfer). A white-box attack on the TGN of 0.05 standard deviations per input cuts its 1-hour score by 19%, 0.1 by 35%, 0.25 by 61% (0.203 to 0.080). Random noise of size 0.1 costs only 0.3%, so the danger is manipulation, not ordinary data errors. The same inputs fed to boosting, which the attacker never saw, do no harm at 1 hour but cost 11 to 33% at 6 hours, because they push the same features (volatility, volume) the same way. Defences (checking inputs across exchanges, mixing in a non-differentiable model, adversarial training) are future work.
      4. 4
        The strangest test days. Number one is 10 October 2025, the liquidation cascade (score 21.3 against 14.4 for the next day). The next nine cluster in late February to early March 2024 and late July to mid August 2026; most were not stress days and none is near a catalogued event, so they are reported as "unclassified": the T-GAE says a day was unusual, not why, and no manipulation type is claimed.

      Three extra checks ran after the notebook, from the same code (their results are in reports/robustness/ and reports/compression/): a 10-step PGD attack was within 0.002 of one-step FGSM at every size, so FGSM was already close to the worst case; driver stability: the top reason stayed the same for 99, 98 and 97% of the 2,000 riskiest alerts under noise of 0.05, 0.1 and 0.25 standard deviations; and int8 compression: 3.1 times smaller but 1.8 times slower, so the full model is served.

      Part 11

      Every technique, explained

      Each method used anywhere in the project, in three lines: plain words, how it works, and what it gave us. Learn the first line of each; read the rest when a question needs it.

      Simple version

      A project like this is a toolbox. Some tools look at one coin, some at pairs of coins, some at the whole network; some are the models, some judge the models, and some try to break them. Here is every tool, one at a time.

      1One coin's price 2Coins moving together 3The coin network 4Contagion baselines 5The learning models 6Training tricks 7Scoring and statistics 8Explaining the model 9The life of a shock 10Stress tests and compression

      1. Looking at one coin's price

      Log returns

      Plain wordsThe percentage change from one hour to the next, written so that changes add up neatly.

      How it worksr = ln(price now ÷ price an hour ago). For small moves it is almost the percentage; unlike percentages, a day's log return is exactly the sum of its 24 hourly ones.

      In our projectEvery input, the shock score and the volatility features start from hourly log returns (the feature log_return).

      Stationarity and the ADF test

      Plain wordsA series is "stationary" if it behaves the same way at any time, like a heartbeat, not like a child's height. Prices drift and grow; returns bounce around zero. Patterns found in drifting series are often fake.

      How it worksThe augmented Dickey–Fuller test starts from the boring assumption "this series wanders off and never comes back" (it has a unit root). A small p-value throws that assumption out, so the series is stationary.

      In our projectLog prices: wandering for 15 of 20 coins. Returns: stationary for all 20. So no model ever sees raw prices (notebook 01, section 4).

      Fat tails: kurtosis and the Jarque–Bera test

      Plain wordsIn a bell curve, giant moves almost never happen. Crypto has far more giant moves than a bell curve allows.

      How it worksKurtosis measures how heavy the tails are (a bell curve scores 0 "excess kurtosis"). Jarque–Bera tests whether the lopsidedness and tail weight of a sample could come from a bell curve.

      In our projectMedian excess kurtosis 18; the bell curve rejected for all 20 coins. So: Huber loss for volatility, inputs capped at ±5, and scores built for rare events (notebook 01, section 5).

      Autocorrelation and volatility clustering

      Plain wordsDoes this hour look like the last one? For direction (up or down): no, it is a coin flip. For jumpiness: yes; calm follows calm and storms follow storms.

      How it worksThe autocorrelation function (ACF) correlates a series with itself 1, 2, 3… steps earlier. On returns it tests direction; on squared returns it tests jumpiness.

      In our projectDirection −0.024 (the same sign as yesterday only 47% of days), jumpiness +0.184. That is why we predict shocks and volatility, never price direction (notebook 01, section 6).

      Rush hours: removing seasonality

      Plain wordsMarkets have rush hours. A move that is normal at 2 pm on a Wednesday is unusual at 6 am on a Sunday, so each hour is judged against its own usual level.

      How it worksOn training data only, measure the typical jumpiness and volume of each of the 168 hours of the week, then divide every hour's return by its slot's typical size.

      In our projectWednesday 12:00 UTC is 2.17× an average hour, Sunday 06:00 is 0.69×; weekends trade 0.77× the weekday volume (notebook 01, section 14).

      The z-score shock rule

      Plain words"How many normal moves big is this move?" Four times bigger than usual is a shock; as big as usual is not.

      How it worksz = this hour's (rush-hour-adjusted) return ÷ the coin's typical move over the previous 168 hours, ending the hour before. |z| above 2.29 is a shock; 2.29 was chosen on training data so that about 4% of hours are shocks.

      In our projectUsing only past hours keeps it honest; the first draft measured each day partly against itself (Part 10, archive table). Daily first version: 2.5 against 30 days.

      Robust scaling (median and IQR, training data only)

      Plain wordsPut every coin on the same ruler, so the model sees "busy for this coin", not "busy in dollars".

      How it worksSubtract the coin's median and divide by its interquartile range (the spread of the middle half of its values), which ignores extreme days. Fitted on training dates only.

      In our projectCoins listed after the training years (ARB, SUI, PEPE, WLD, JUP, ENA) use the scaler fitted on all training coins (notebook 01, sections 8 and 13).

      Huber loss and clipping

      Plain wordsIf a few huge errors decide how a model learns, one crash day can hijack training. Huber loss counts small errors normally but big ones gently; clipping caps extreme inputs.

      How it worksSquared error inside a small range, straight-line error outside it. Clipping limits each scaled input to between −5 and +5.

      In our projectThe LSTM volatility model trains with Huber loss; the shock models use focal loss instead.

      Removing duplicate inputs

      Plain wordsTwo inputs that say the same thing waste the model's effort.

      How it worksOn training data, if two features have a rank correlation above 0.9, keep one.

      In our projectAt 1 hour the 7-day volatility repeated the 3-day one (0.90), so it went: 6 market features. All 8 network features stayed (highest pair 0.78).

      2. How coins move together

      Correlation

      Plain wordsDo two coins go up and down at the same time? +1 = always together, 0 = unrelated, −1 = always opposite.

      How it worksPearson correlation of returns over a window: 30 days on daily data, 168 hours on hourly data.

      In our projectEvery daily pair is positive (0.39 to 0.80, average 0.57), and higher after crises (0.61 against 0.54). Shared movement is the reason for a graph model (notebook 01, section 10).

      The Epps effect

      Plain wordsMeasured over very short intervals, related prices can look less related, because one reacts a few minutes late and its move lands in the next interval.

      How it worksCompare the average correlation measured over 1, 2, 4, 12 and 24-hour intervals (Epps, 1979).

      In our projectWeak in crypto: 0.58 at 1 hour, 0.60 at 2, 0.61 at 4, 0.62 at 12, 0.59 at a day. Coins trade around the clock on busy exchanges, so prices catch up within the hour (notebook 01, section 16; report section III-C).

      Cross-correlation and lead-lag

      Plain wordsIf Bitcoin moves now, does Ethereum move an hour later? If so, Bitcoin "leads".

      How it worksCorrelate the leader at hour t with the other coin at hour t + k, for every k from −24 to +24, on returns divided by recent volatility so crash hours do not dominate.

      In our projectStrong in the same hour (0.65 with BTC, 0.68 with ETH), tiny at every other lag (0.035), with the biggest values at +24 hours: a daily rhythm, not a leader (notebook 01, section 15).

      The circular-shift null

      Plain wordsTo know what counts as "more than chance", build fake data in which the timing link is broken on purpose, and see how big correlations get there.

      How it worksRotate one coin's series by a random offset of at least 30 days (the end wraps round to the start). Each coin keeps its own habits, such as calm and stormy spells, but any real timing link is destroyed. Repeat 500 times; the 99th percentile of the largest correlation is the chance line.

      In our projectMost pairs beat the line, but only through the 24-hour rhythm, which survives a shift. So the line is only an upper limit, and Granger tests settle the question.

      Granger causality

      Plain words"Does Bitcoin's last few hours help predict Ethereum, beyond what Ethereum's own last few hours already tell us?" It tests extra predictive power, not true cause and effect.

      How it worksPredict the coin from its own past 3 hours; then add the leader's past 3 hours. If the prediction improves by more than luck allows, the leader "Granger-causes" the coin. The gain in R² is the size of the effect.

      In our projectAfter correction only 8 of 45 leader-to-coin and 10 of 45 coin-to-leader tests pass, so neither direction leads. The biggest gain is 0.47% (ETH to QNT), the median 0.02%.

      Many tests at once: Benjamini–Hochberg

      Plain wordsRun 90 tests and a few will look "significant" by pure luck, like a streak of heads.

      How it worksSort the p-values and raise the bar so that, of the results you call significant, at most about 5% are flukes (the "false discovery rate"). It is milder than Bonferroni, which guards against even one fluke and would hide real effects.

      In our projectApplied to the 90 Granger tests.

      Replication and the lead-lag decision rule

      Plain wordsA real pattern should show up again in new data.

      How it worksMeasure the one-hour-later pattern on training data, measure it again on validation, and correlate the two.

      In our projectIt does not repeat (−0.11 for BTC, −0.40 for ETH). Our rule for adding "who leads" arrows to the graph: beat the null, go one way only, add at least 1% of R², and replicate. It failed three of the four, so the graph has no such arrows.

      Eigenvalues, the market mode, and the Marchenko–Pastur bound

      Plain wordsLook for the main "directions" in which 30 coins move. One direction dominates: everything moving together, "the market".

      How it worksThe 30 eigenvalues of the correlation table add up to 30; each says how much of all the movement one direction explains. Random-matrix theory (the Marchenko–Pastur law) gives the biggest eigenvalue that pure noise could produce for this many coins and hours: here 1.08.

      In our projectThe biggest is 19.3, so the market explains 64% of hourly movement, and it is the only one above the noise line (notebook 02, section 1; report section IV-D, citing Zarifian and colleagues).

      VAR models and BIC

      Plain wordsPredict each coin's next return from the recent returns of all coins at once.

      How it worksA vector autoregression (VAR) is a set of straight-line formulas, one per coin, each using the last p hours of every coin. BIC picks p by rewarding a good fit and charging for every extra number.

      In our projectBIC chose 1 hour back on hourly data; the daily cross-check uses 2 days.

      Diebold–Yilmaz spillover: TO, FROM, NET and the spillover index

      Plain words"How much of coin A's surprise comes from coin B?" Add it up over all pairs and you know who mostly sends shocks and who mostly receives them.

      How it worksFrom the VAR, a forecast-error variance decomposition (FEVD) splits each coin's 24-hour forecast uncertainty into shares caused by each coin. TO = what a coin sends, FROM = what it receives, NET = TO minus FROM, and the total spillover index (TSI) = the average share coming from other coins. "Generalized" (Pesaran and Shin, 1998) means the answer does not depend on the order the coins are listed in.

      In our projectHourly TSI 91.6%, but hourly NET ranks coins exactly like eigenvector centrality (1.00): at hourly speed it measures how tied a coin is to the market. Daily: TSI 86.0%, ETH the top sender; daily and hourly rankings agree only weakly (0.21) (notebook 02, sections 2 and 2b).

      Cholesky against generalized (the first draft's mistake)

      Plain wordsThe older recipe gives a different answer depending on which coin you list first, like a race in which lane 1 always gets a head start.

      How it worksCholesky assumes the first coin can affect the others within the same period but not the other way round; the generalized version makes no such assumption.

      In our projectThe first draft used Cholesky with Bitcoin in column 1: Bitcoin kept 97% of its own variance and looked like a giant sender (index 54.2%, NET +731.5). Generalized: index 86.0%, ETH on top.

      3. The coin network

      Correlation network with a fitted threshold

      Plain wordsDraw a line between two coins when their last week of returns moved strongly together. Too many lines and everything is joined to everything, which tells you nothing.

      How it worksJoin coins whose 168-hour correlation is at or above a threshold chosen on training data so that about 30% of all possible lines exist: 0.75 at 1 hour, 0.77 at 2 hours. Each coin also keeps its 2 strongest lines, so none is left alone.

      In our projectThe old draft value of 0.5 joined 75% of pairs, a near-complete "hairball". Final density 30 to 39% (notebook 01, section 16).

      Network shape: density, clustering, path length, k-core

      Plain wordsSimple ways to describe a network: how many lines there are, whether a coin's friends are also friends with each other, how many hops separate coins, and how big the tightest inner circle is.

      How it worksDensity = lines present ÷ lines possible. Clustering = share of a coin's neighbour pairs that are joined. Shortest path = average hops between coins. Largest k-core = the biggest group in which every coin has at least k lines inside the group.

      In our projectThe test-period network is denser and more clustered than training (0.35 against 0.30; 0.57 against 0.47), and both rise after crises (notebook 02, section 6).

      Minimum spanning tree and tree length

      Plain wordsThe market's skeleton: the fewest, strongest lines that still connect all 30 coins, with no loops.

      How it worksTurn correlation into a distance, d = √(2(1 − ρ)): 0 for coins that move identically, 1.41 for unrelated ones, 2 for opposites (Mantegna, 1999). Keep the 29 shortest lines that join everything. The average line length (Onnela and colleagues, 2003) shrinks when the market moves as one block.

      In our projectStrongest lines DOGE–SHIB and BTC–ETH. DOT has the most lines (11), but to loners. The tree averages 0.66 and shrinks in every crisis (0.36 at its lowest), exactly when spreading money across coins stops protecting you (notebook 02, section 5).

      Strength and eigenvector centrality

      Plain wordsStrength: how strongly a coin is tied to everyone on average. Eigenvector centrality: you are important if you are tied to important coins.

      How it worksStrength = average correlation with the other coins. Eigenvector centrality = the coin's share of the biggest eigenvalue's direction in the correlation network; both scaled so the average coin is 1.

      In our projectTop on the test window: LINK, DOT, AVAX, ADA, ETH. Both are model inputs.

      PageRank on a "depends on" network

      Plain wordsGoogle's old idea: a random surfer clicks from page to page; the pages where the surfer spends most time matter most.

      How it worksUsed only on networks with arrows. The arrow points from coin i to coin j when i is driven by j, so importance collects at the coins others depend on. Scaled so the average is 1.

      In our projectOn the spillover network it copies the NET ranking (1.00). On the shock-transmission network it differs (0.73): ETC, XRP, POL, AVAX and ADA on top, Bitcoin only 23rd (notebook 02, section 3).

      Shock-transmission network and shock lift

      Plain wordsCount how often coin j has a shock within an hour after coin i has one, and compare with how often j shocks anyway. The extra is the "lift".

      How it worksOver the previous 90 days: the chance that j shocks within 1 hour after i did, minus j's normal rate. Needs at least 5 shocks of i. The only network built from time order.

      In our projectGives two model inputs, shock PageRank and shock out-lift (the average extra chance that other coins shock right after this one).

      Louvain communities and modularity

      Plain wordsFind groups of coins that are more tied to each other than to the rest, like friend groups in a class. First take out "the whole market moved", or everything is one big group.

      How it worksRemove the market direction from the correlations, then let the Louvain algorithm (Blondel and colleagues, 2008) merge coins into groups to maximise modularity: lines inside groups minus what chance would give (0 = no group structure). A fixed seed makes it repeatable.

      In our projectFive groups: meme coins; BTC, ETH, DeFi and BNB; newer high-beta platforms; old payment coins; a group led by DOT and ETC. Modularity 0.41 to 0.46, falling in crises (notebook 02, section 4).

      NMI and the shuffle test

      Plain wordsHow much do two ways of sorting the same coins agree, and could that much agreement happen by chance?

      How it worksNormalised mutual information is 0 for unrelated sortings and 1 for identical ones. Shuffle the sector labels 2,000 times; the share of shuffles that agree as much is the p-value.

      In our project0.47 against 0.32 for shuffles, p = 0.003: real but partial. Coins group by how they trade as much as by what they do.

      Assortativity

      Plain wordsDo coins mostly link to their own kind (the same sector), or to others?

      How it worksA correlation between the sectors at the two ends of each line: positive = mostly same sector, negative = mostly different.

      In our projectSlightly negative (−0.14, then −0.05). Contagion is the same inside and across sectors (9.3% against 9.3%), except meme coins (13.7% against 10.9%) (notebook 02, section 7).

      Event studies and case studies

      Plain wordsLine up the hours around a known event and look closely at what happened, in order.

      How it worksCut a window around each crisis (24 hours before to 72 after) and follow returns, shock counts or network measures hour by hour, compared with the weeks before.

      In our projectFTX: FTT fell 14 standard deviations on 6 November with nobody following; on 8 November 18 to 24 coins shocked in the same hours as FTT's biggest falls (notebook 02, section 8; notebook 05, section 1).

      4. Contagion baselines

      Independent cascade

      Plain wordsLike a cold going round a class: each sick child gets one chance to infect each other child, with a chance that depends on the pair. A child stays healthy only if every sick child misses.

      How it worksKempe, Kleinberg and Tardos (2003). For each pair, pij = how often j shocked within k hours after i shocked, on training data, pulled towards j's normal rate as if we had 10 extra observations at that rate (so rare pairs get no wild numbers). With several coins shocked at once: chance(j) = 1 − (1 − p1j) × (1 − p2j) × …

      In our projectTest AUPRC 0.159 at 1 hour against 0.105 for random. Its pair numbers alone do no better than the base rate, so its gain comes from counting how many coins shocked at once. About 900 numbers, so it cannot memorise (notebook 02, section 9).

      Base rate

      Plain wordsThe laziest sensible guess: each coin's usual chance of shocking.

      How it worksGive every event the receiving coin's training-period shock rate. It scores about the positive rate, which is why the positive rate is the "random" line.

      In our project0.106 at 1 hour on test.

      5. The learning models

      TGN (the main model)

      Plain wordsA diary for every coin, a habit of looking at each coin's 10 latest events, and a small judge that reads both coins' situation and gives 5 chances.

      How it worksRossi and colleagues (2020): GRU memory of 64 numbers per coin, attention with 2 heads over 10 recent events, prediction head 161 → 64 → 5. 70,021 parameters.

      In our projectEverything about it is in Part 7.

      DyGFormer

      Plain wordsA second opinion with no diary: every time, it rereads each coin's latest history from scratch.

      How it worksYu and colleagues (NeurIPS 2023). For each event it takes the last 16 interactions of both coins, notes who they were with, when, and how often the same partners appear on both sides (neighbour co-occurrence), cuts the sequence into pieces of 2 and runs a small Transformer (2 layers, 2 heads, width 32). Our compact rebuild: 24,021 parameters. The official library was not used because its random "negative" examples and fraction-based splits would break our no-peeking rules.

      In our projectTied with the TGN on test at every window (0.208 against 0.205 at 1 hour) (notebook 03, section 4b).

      Gradient boosting (histogram trees)

      Plain wordsA team of small decision trees ("if volume is unusual and jumpiness is high, risk goes up"); each new tree fixes the mistakes of the trees before it.

      How it worksscikit-learn's histogram gradient boosting, which sorts each input into ranges to train fast. Learning rate 0.05, at most 31 leaves per tree, at least 200 events per leaf; trees added 50 at a time (up to 1,000) until validation stops improving. Trees per window: 100, 150, 50, 200, 150.

      In our projectSame inputs as the TGN's head plus shock state and market breadth. 0.178 on test at 1 hour; it memorised its training years (0.64 on training against 0.155 on validation).

      LSTM

      Plain wordsReads one coin's last week hour by hour, keeping a running memory. It never sees other coins.

      How it worksHochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997): 1 layer, 32 memory units, dropout 0.2, 5,665 parameters; 168-hour windows of 6 features; focal loss for shocks, Huber loss for volatility.

      In our projectNext-hour shock: 0.109, 2.8 times random. Volatility: beaten by HAR-RV.

      HAR-RV

      Plain wordsFuture jumpiness is roughly a weighted mix of the last hour's, the last day's and the last week's jumpiness. Simple and very hard to beat.

      How it worksCorsi (2009): a straight-line formula, future volatility = a + b × last hour + c × last 24 hours + d × last 168 hours, with a to d fitted by least squares on training data.

      In our projectBeats the LSTM at every window (test error 5.0 against 5.5 × 10−5 at 1 hour).

      Logistic regression on simple rules

      Plain wordsOne weighted sum of a few numbers, squeezed into a chance.

      How it worksFitted on training data; each weight says how much a rule pushes the risk.

      In our projectNext-hour shock with 4 rules (how unusual the move is now, shock now, own shocks in the last day, market breadth): 0.095. In the final review a 4-feature version matched the TGN on contagion (Part 9).

      Graph convolution (GCN)

      Plain wordsEach coin updates its numbers by listening to its neighbours, louder for stronger links, then reshapes the result with learned knobs.

      How it worksOne layer computes, for every node, a weighted average of its own and its neighbours' numbers, then applies a learned linear map. Two layers let news travel two hops.

      In our projectThe first two layers of the T-GAE (14 → 32 → 16 numbers per coin).

      Temporal graph autoencoder (T-GAE)

      Plain wordsLearns what a normal day of the coin network looks like and tries to predict tomorrow. If tomorrow looks very different from what it expected, the day is "surprising": an anomaly.

      How it worksFor each daily network: two GCN layers, a GRU carrying each coin's state from day to day, and two decoders, one guessing tomorrow's links and one guessing each coin's 14 activity numbers. The anomaly score is the prediction error. 3,646 parameters, trained on 2020 to 2022, 5 seeds.

      In our projectBarely separates stress days (0.511 against 0.893 for plain turbulence); it did rank 10 October 2025 as the strangest test day (notebook 06).

      Ensembles: of seeds and of models

      Plain wordsAsk several experts and combine their opinions.

      How it worksSeed ensemble: average the 5 runs' chances. Model ensemble: turn each model's scores into ranks (so they share one scale), then a weighted average with weights picked per window on validation.

      In our projectSeeds: +0.001. Models: better than the TGN at 24 hours (+0.008), worse at 1 to 3 hours.

      6. Training tricks

      Focal loss

      Plain wordsA teacher who spends little time on questions everyone gets right and more on the hard, rare ones.

      How it worksLin and colleagues (2017): loss = −α (1 − p)γ log p, where p is the chance given to the right answer. When p is close to 1 (easy), (1 − p)γ is tiny, so the example barely counts. γ = 2; α = 0.25 for "yes" events and 0.75 for "no" events.

      In our projectUsed by the TGN, DyGFormer and the shock LSTM (report equation 8).

      Early stopping, patience and a minimum number of epochs

      Plain wordsStop studying when mock-exam scores stop improving, keep your best version, and do not quit after one lucky early test.

      How it worksAfter each epoch, score validation AUPRC at 1 hour; keep the best; stop after 10 epochs without improvement; for DyGFormer and the no-memory model, never before epoch 30.

      In our projectThe first final runs (30 epochs, patience 4) stopped while still improving: 0.138, then 0.153 with the longer budget.

      Weight decay and dropout

      Plain wordsTwo ways to stop memorising: keep the knobs small, and switch random neurons off while learning so the model cannot lean on any one.

      How it worksWeight decay adds a small pull towards zero on every knob (0.0001); dropout zeroes a random 20% of the numbers at each step.

      In our projectMore of both (dropout 0.4, weight decay 0.001) or a smaller network only made validation worse (0.143 and 0.133 against 0.153).

      Seeds and reproducibility

      Plain wordsTraining starts from random numbers; the seed fixes them so a run can be repeated exactly.

      How it works5 seeds (0 to 4) per final model, deterministic settings, every run logged with its git commit.

      In our projectSeeds agree within about ±0.003 (±0.005 in development runs); a replay of the final TGN reproduces its stored test score within 0.0007.

      Refitting on training plus validation

      Plain wordsAfter choosing the settings, train once more on more data before the final exam.

      How it worksRetrain on everything up to December 2023, with the number of epochs (77) or trees fixed in advance, because there is no mock exam left to stop on.

      In our projectHelped boosting (0.178 to 0.185), hurt the TGN (0.205 to 0.188). Not re-tuned, because that would be tuning on the test.

      Calibration (binned isotonic)

      Plain wordsMaking chances honest: of all the times the page says 30%, about 30% should come true.

      How it worksOn validation data only, group events by score (at least 500 per group) and fit an always-rising curve from score to real frequency; chances are also forced to grow with the window (1 hour ≤ 3 hours ≤ … ≤ 24 hours).

      In our projectUsed by the dashboard (Part 14). The report notes calibration was not checked on the test period.

      7. Scoring and statistics

      AUPRC, AUROC, positive rate and lift

      Plain wordsAUPRC: how well real "yes" events are pushed to the top of the list; random scores the share of "yes" events. AUROC: the chance a random "yes" is ranked above a random "no" (0.5 = coin flip). Lift: a score divided by the random score.

      How it worksAUPRC is computed as average precision (report equation 9): walk down the list, and at each new "yes" found, add (gain in recall) × (precision so far).

      In our projectTGN 0.205 against 0.105 at 1 hour (lift 1.95); AUROC 0.64 to 0.69. Slowly explained in Part 5.

      Precision at the top

      Plain wordsOf the alerts the model was most sure about, how many came true?

      How it worksSort test events by predicted risk, take the top 1% (or 5%, 10%), and count the share that really were followed by contagion.

      In our projectTop 1%: 32% within 1 hour (random 10.5%), 61% within 3, 76% within 6.

      Day-block bootstrap and p-values

      Plain wordsCould the win be luck? Rebuild the test period 1,000 times from randomly drawn whole days and see how often the winner still wins.

      How it worksDraw 971 test days with replacement; recompute both models' AUPRC on the same events; repeat 1,000 times (report equation 10). The middle 95% of the differences is the interval; the share of rebuilds where the difference is zero or below is the one-sided p-value. Whole days, because one crash creates hundreds of linked events.

      In our projectTGN against cascade: +0.046 (0.019 to 0.074) at 1 hour; no rebuild favoured the cascade.

      Training-to-validation lift ratio (an overfitting check)

      Plain wordsIf one student aces the homework but slips much more on new questions than a student who cannot memorise, the first one memorised.

      How it worksLift on training ÷ lift on validation. The cascade cannot memorise, so its ratio (about 1.1) is just the market changing.

      In our projectTGN about 1.5 at 1 hour: some overfitting at short windows, none at 24 hours (notebook 03, section 5).

      Mean squared error

      Plain wordsThe average of the squared misses; lower is better. Squaring makes big misses count extra.

      How it worksCompare predicted and real volatility over the next 1, 6 and 24 hours, against HAR-RV, "same as last period", the coin's average and zero.

      In our projectHAR-RV lowest at every window.

      8. Explaining the model

      Integrated gradients and completeness

      Plain wordsStart from an "average situation" and slide step by step to the real one, writing down how much each input pushes the score along the way. The pushes add up to the whole change.

      How it worksSundararajan and colleagues (2017): add up the model's gradient along a straight path from the baseline (the training average) to the real input. Completeness: the pushes sum exactly to the difference between the real score and the baseline score; we check it every run (largest gap 0.009).

      In our projectReceiver 65%, source 25%, event 10%; the top single reason is the receiver's volume against its past week (25%).

      TempME

      Plain wordsLooks for small patterns of past events ("motifs", such as a → b then b → c) around the two coins of an alert, and scores which recent events mattered.

      How it worksChen and Ying (NeurIPS 2023): sample motifs of up to 3 steps around both coins, learn an importance score for each with an information bottleneck (below), and apply the scores as weights on the TGN's attention. Trained on 10,000 validation events with the official settings.

      In our projectBarely better than random (0.157 against 0.152), because there are no special chains to find.

      Information bottleneck

      Plain wordsExplain with as little as possible: an explanation that keeps everything explains nothing.

      How it worksThe explainer is rewarded for keeping the prediction and charged for how much it keeps, pulled towards keeping about 30% of events ("prior sparsity 0.3"); β = 0.5 sets the balance and a temperature of 0.07 makes keep-or-drop almost a yes/no choice.

      In our projectPart of TempME.

      Fidelity and the characterization score

      Plain wordsA good explanation passes two tests: the chosen events alone are enough (keep only them and the prediction stays), and they are needed (remove them and it changes).

      How it worksFidelity− = change when keeping only the chosen events (lower is better); fidelity+ = change when removing them (higher is better); both divided by the change from removing all recent events. Characterization combines them (a harmonic mean, as in GraphFramEx, Amara and colleagues, 2022).

      In our projectWith 3 events kept: TempME 0.157, random 0.152, attention 0.147, most recent 0.192, a mask tuned for every single alert 0.248.

      Attention entropy

      Plain wordsIs the model's attention focused on a few events, or spread evenly like butter on toast?

      How it worksNormalised entropy of the attention weights: 0 = all weight on one event, 1 = perfectly even.

      In our project1.000: the TGN averages its 10 recent events instead of picking some.

      Time-shuffled null for motifs

      Plain wordsIf real event patterns look like patterns in a stream with shuffled times, there is no special pattern.

      How it worksShuffle the event times to break their order, count the motif types in both streams, compare.

      In our project83% against 86% of motifs are three-step walks through four different coins; only walks that return to a coin are more common in real data (5.9% against 3.3%).

      Plausibility with Spearman correlation

      Plain wordsDo the coins an explanation credits match the coins network analysis calls central?

      How it worksEach top alert gives credit to the coins of its 3 most important events; compare the credit ranking with the network rankings by rank correlation, also after dividing by how often each coin appears at all ("lift over exposure").

      In our projectTempME runs against the rankings (about −0.8), so its credit is not evidence of who spreads contagion.

      The rebuild check

      Plain wordsBefore explaining a model, prove you are explaining the real one.

      How it worksCapture the inputs the TGN used for each event, recompute its output from them, and compare with the stored output.

      In our projectLargest difference 2.4 × 10−7.

      9. The life of a shock

      Fresh shocks and breadth types

      Plain wordsCount each storm once, and sort storms by how many coins they hit at once.

      How it worksA fresh shock has no shock of the same coin in the previous 24 hours. Breadth = share of the other coins shocking in the same hour: market-wide 30% or more, partial 10 to 30%, isolated (idiosyncratic) under 10%; plus crash or pump.

      In our project18,171 fresh shocks out of 57,792 shock hours (report Table V).

      Hours back to normal

      Plain wordsHow long until a shocked coin is as calm as it was before?

      How it worksFor each fresh shock, the first hour after which the coin's volatility is back at its level before the shock; report the median, with intervals from redrawing whole days.

      In our project8 to 10 hours for every type; 87% within a day.

      Re-shocks, followers and continuation

      Plain wordsAfter a shock: does the same coin shock again, how many other coins follow, and does the price keep going or bounce back?

      How it worksRe-shock = another shock of the same coin within 24 hours. Followers = other coins shocking within 6 hours. Continuation = the return after the shock, in units of the coin's volatility, signed so that positive means "the move continued".

      In our projectRe-shock 55 to 65%; followers 8.1 after a market-wide crash against 4.5 to 5.1 after an isolated shock; direction mostly unpredictable.

      Matched null and half-life

      Plain wordsCompare the hours after a shock with the hours after an ordinary calm hour of the same coin in the same month, then ask how fast the difference halves.

      How it worksExtra jumpiness over the next 72 hours minus the same profile after a random calm hour; the half-life is the first hour at which the extra is half its value at hour 1.

      In our projectAbout 2.5 hours in every sector, with no sector differences.

      Blast radius of a crisis

      Plain wordsHow many coins did a crisis hit, how much more often than usual, and how fast?

      How it worksStart each crisis at the hour of its date with the most shocks; count the coins hit within 72 hours and compare the shock rate with the week before.

      In our projectAll 9 crises were market-wide and reached every coin; LUNA raised the shock rate most (+0.19), the ETF approval least (+0.01).

      10. Stress tests and compression

      Random input noise

      Plain wordsShake the inputs a little and see if the answers wobble.

      How it worksAdd random bell-curve noise of size σ to both coins' features and the event's numbers. Inputs are standardised, so σ = 0.5 means half a typical deviation.

      In our projectσ = 0.5: TGN −5.7%, boosting −4.7%. The TGN's chances move half as much.

      Missing events

      Plain wordsPretend part of the data feed never arrived.

      How it worksA random share of test events never reaches the TGN's memory and recent lists; every event is still scored.

      In our projectUp to 50% missing: under 0.5% change.

      FGSM attack

      Plain wordsThe worst small nudge: an attacker who knows the model pushes every input a little, exactly in the direction that hurts it most.

      How it worksFast gradient sign method (Goodfellow and colleagues, 2015): new input = input + ε × (the sign of the loss gradient), using the true answers. "White-box": the attacker sees everything.

      In our projectε = 0.1 cuts the 1-hour score by about 35%; random noise of the same size costs under 1%.

      Transfer attack

      Plain wordsUse inputs crafted to fool one model on a different model the attacker never saw.

      How it worksFeed the TGN's FGSM inputs to gradient boosting.

      In our projectNo harm at 1 hour, 11 to 33% lower at 6 hours.

      PGD attack

      Plain wordsA more patient attacker: many small nudges instead of one big one.

      How it worksProjected gradient descent: 10 FGSM steps from a random start, each time pulled back inside the allowed ±ε box.

      In our projectWithin 0.002 of FGSM at every size, so FGSM was already close to the worst case.

      Driver stability

      Plain wordsIf inputs wobble a little, does the explanation still name the same reasons?

      How it worksRecompute the integrated-gradient reasons under noise; compare the top reason, and the top-5 lists by Jaccard similarity (shared items ÷ all items).

      In our projectThe top reason stays for 99, 98 and 97% of the 2,000 riskiest alerts at noise 0.05, 0.1 and 0.25.

      Stress days and turbulence

      Plain wordsAn outside yardstick for "the market went wild today", taken from the returns, not from any model.

      How it worksStress day = at least one hour in which 70% or more of the coins shocked (20.7% of days). Turbulence = the day's average |z| across coins.

      In our projectTurbulence spots stress days well (0.893); the T-GAE does not (0.511).

      Quantisation and pruning

      Plain wordsMake the model file smaller by storing its numbers with fewer digits (quantisation) or by deleting the weakest connections (pruning).

      How it worksint8 quantisation stores knobs as 8-bit whole numbers instead of 32-bit decimals; 50% pruning sets the smallest half of the knobs to zero.

      In our projectint8: 3.1 times smaller but 1.8 times slower on CPU and 0.004 to 0.007 less AUPRC; pruning: no gain. The dashboard serves the full model.

      The abstract in plain words

      Most studies of crypto contagion measure how strongly coins move together; they do not predict which coin is hit next. We turned that into a prediction task: when coin i has a shock, will coin j have one within 1, 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours? We built a fair, no-peeking test from Binance hourly data (30 coins, 2020 to 2026, 1.46 million events) and raced a TGN against DyGFormer, gradient boosting, an LSTM, an independent cascade and classic finance baselines. On 2024 to 2026 the TGN scores 0.205 at 1 hour (random 0.105, cascade 0.159) and 0.739 at 24 hours. It beats the cascade everywhere and boosting at 1 to 3 hours (borderline); DyGFormer ties it; memory helps only from 6 hours. The signal is mostly the receiving coin's current state. A deliberate attack cuts the score by about 35%; noise and missing data do little. Negative results and every test-period score are reported.

      I. Introduction

      • Motivation: crypto trades around the clock and reacts violently to news; LUNA (May 2022), FTX (November 2022) and 10 October 2025 each pulled most big coins down within hours. A trader, an exchange's risk desk or a regulator wants to know which coins will be hit next, and how soon. Classic measures (correlations, spillover indices) describe average links; they do not say who is next. Daily data mash several shocks and their order into one number; hourly data keep the order.
      • Problem statement: coin i shocks at hour t; for every other active coin j, predict whether j shocks within k hours, k ∈ {1, 3, 6, 12, 24} ("∈" means "is one of"). Only 10.5% of events are "yes" at 1 hour, and everything depends on time, so the model must know who interacts with whom, and the test must never leak the future.
      • Why deep learning: a coin's risk depends on its own state, on the shocking coin's state and on their recent history together. A TGN keeps a learned memory per coin that every event updates, which matches this; an LSTM sees one series only, and trees see one fixed snapshot of features. Whether the extra machinery is worth it is tested against strong baselines with significance tests.

      B. Cleaning and the labels

      On daily data, prices fail the stationarity test and log returns pass, so all models use log returns; returns have fat tails (excess kurtosis about 18), so inputs are robust-scaled with training medians and interquartile ranges, and number targets use Huber loss. Hourly jumpiness follows the clock (0.69 to 2.17 times its average by hour of the week), so a training-period profile is divided out. Everything fitted (scalers, rush-hour profile, graph thresholds, the shock threshold) uses training data only, and an event is scored only if its whole answer window lies inside its own period. Labels that count the same hour were rejected, because they would call simultaneous co-movement contagion. Returns barely correlate with their own past while their sizes do, so the project predicts shocks and volatility, never direction.

      The ten equations in plain words
      #The equationWhat it says
      1zi,t = r̃i,t / σ̂i,t−1The shock score: this hour's rush-hour-adjusted return (r̃) divided by the coin's typical hourly move (σ̂) over the 168 hours up to the hour before.
      2Si,t = 1[ |zi,t| > θ ]The shock flag: 1 if the size of z is above θ = 2.29, otherwise 0. "1[…]" means "1 if true, 0 if false".
      3y(k)i→j,t = 1[ Σu=t+1…t+k Sj,u ≥ 1 ]The answer: 1 if coin j has at least one shock in hours t+1 to t+k. The sum starts at t+1, so the same hour never counts.
      4mi(t) = [ si(t⁻) ‖ sj(t⁻) ‖ eij(t) ‖ φ(Δti) ]The message for coin i's diary: glue together i's diary just before t (64 numbers), j's diary (64), the event's 5 numbers, and a 16-number time code of how long since i's diary last changed. 149 numbers in all.
      5si(t) = GRU( si(t⁻), mi(t) )The diary update: the GRU reads the old diary and the message and writes the new diary.
      6hi = Attn( si, {su, eiu, φ(Δtu)} for u in N10(i) )The coin's current summary (64 numbers): attention over its 10 most recent partners u, using their diaries, the events and how long ago they happened.
      7p̂ij = σ( W2 ReLU( W1 [ hi ‖ hj ‖ xi ‖ xj ‖ eij ] ) )The prediction: glue both summaries (64 + 64), both coins' 14 features (14 + 14) and the event's 5 numbers (161 in all), pass them through a 64-neuron layer with ReLU (W1) and an output layer (W2), and squeeze the 5 outputs into chances with the sigmoid σ.
      8FL(pt) = −αt (1 − pt)γ log ptFocal loss: pt is the chance the model gave to the right answer. Easy cases (pt near 1) are shrunk by (1 − pt)γ; γ = 2, α = 0.25 for "yes", 0.75 for "no".
      9AP = Σn (Rn − Rn−1) PnAUPRC as average precision: walk down the ranked list; each time recall goes up, add that step times the precision at that point.
      10Δ(b) = APA(D(b)) − APB(D(b))The bootstrap: in rebuild b of the test days (D(b)), the difference between model A's and model B's score. The middle 95% of 1,000 such differences is the interval.
      Say it in the viva

      Equation 4 is the one most worth practising: "Each time a coin is involved in an event, we build a message from both coins' memories, the event's five numbers and a time code; equation 5 then lets a GRU write that message into the coin's 64-number memory. Equations 6 and 7 read the memories back out through attention and a small two-layer network to give five chances."

      C. Network analysis

      Coins are the actors; moving together, or passing on shocks, are the ties. The report lists: correlation networks with a threshold fitted for 30% density (0.75 at 1 hour), minimum spanning trees, generalized spillover networks on 30-day windows, a shock-transmission network with arrows, PageRank and eigenvector centrality, Louvain communities compared with sectors by NMI, and the independent cascade as a baseline. Eight node features per daily snapshot join the six market features to give the 14-number coin description, always taken from the latest snapshot at or before the hour. The Epps effect is weak (0.58 at 1 hour to 0.62 at 12 hours).

      F. How models are scored

      Accuracy is meaningless (always saying "no" is about 90% accurate at 1 hour), so the main score is average precision (equation 9), with AUROC, the precision of the riskiest 1% and lift alongside. Model differences use the day-block bootstrap (equation 10) over 971 test days and 1,000 rebuilds, with both models scored on exactly the same events. Neural models enter the bootstrap as five-seed ensembles (the average of the five chances), while tables of single numbers report the average over seeds. All tuning used validation only, and every configuration scored on the test period is listed in Table VI.

      F. Limitations, as the report lists them

      • Overall discrimination is modest (AUROC 0.64 to 0.69): most shocks are news-driven and hit the whole market in the same hour.
      • One market and one test period; a second, rolling test window is the clean fix.
      • Survivorship bias: the 30 coins were picked from a 2026 ranking.
      • The "garden of forking paths": several versions were scored on the test after earlier results were known. Table VI lists all of them, and the main model was fixed from validation evidence before the refit and never switched for a better test score.
      • Early stopping looks at the 1-hour score only, though memory helps most at longer windows.
      • Calibration was not checked on the test period; no money-based evaluation; no social-media or order-book data.
      • The final TGN, DyGFormer, LSTM and boosting runs come from clean commits; the T-GAE, robustness and prediction runs had uncommitted changes, which the run registry flags.

      The stages in plain words

      1. Choose coins (cryptograph-data --stage audit): apply the four rules from Part 3 to the top 60 coins and keep 30.

      3. Clean (--stage clean): quality checks, short gaps filled and flagged, long gaps masked, renames joined, USDT peg flagged, 2-hour bars made, cross-check against the daily data.
      4. Build (--stage build): features, time-of-day pattern, scaling, shocks, labels, splits, correlation graphs and the 1.46 million-event stream.
      5. Network (--stage network): the 8 daily network features, coin rankings and the independent-cascade baseline.
      6. Train (cryptograph-train --config configs/experiment/<name>.yaml --seed n): every run writes its own folder in runs/: the exact settings, environment, git commit, log, scores, saved model and per-event predictions.
      7. Evaluate (notebook 03): tables, bootstrap tests, alert quality.
      8. Explain and stress-test (notebooks 04 to 06): integrated gradients, TempME, case studies, persistence, attacks, the anomaly model.
      9. Serve: cryptograph-predict scores the latest shocks in the data and saves each coin's chances and reasons; a second run (configs/experiment/predict_events_1h.yaml) does the same for the 9 catalogued crises, for the Past crises tab.
      10. Dashboard: streamlit run dashboard/app.py shows it all (Part 14).

      Limitations and future work

      Name the weaknesses yourself. It shows you understand the work.

      Limitations

      • Modest discrimination (AUROC 0.64 to 0.69): most shocks are news-driven and hit the whole market in the same hour.
      • One market, one test period: Binance, prices in USDT, 2024 to 2026.
      • Survivorship bias: coins were chosen by their 2026 ranking, so coins that died earlier are missing (apart from LUNC and FTT as case studies).
      • Several versions touched the test period (all disclosed in the report's Table VI); the main model was fixed from validation evidence and never switched for a better test score.
      • Model choice used the 1-hour score only, although memory helps most at longer windows.
      • The label depends only on the receiving coin (Part 4), so "who spread it to whom" is not learned directly.
      • A simple model is competitive at hourly resolution (Part 9).
      • No money-based test yet: we have not measured whether acting on alerts reduces losses.
      • No news, social-media or order-book data.
      • The dashboard is not live: it shows the newest prediction on the research data, which ends on 27 September 2026.

      Future work

      • A second, rolling test period and other exchanges.
      • Order-book, funding and minute-level data, where "who moves first" may be visible.
      • Direction-aware labels ("does j crash after i crashes?").
      • News and social-media sentiment as inputs.
      • A money-based backtest: for example, step out of coins flagged "much higher than usual" for 6 hours and measure the reduction in losses and volatility.
      • Defences against manipulation: adversarial training and cross-checking inputs from several sources.
      • Automatic hourly updates for the dashboard: fetch each new Binance hour, rebuild it with the frozen pipeline, check nothing in the past changed, predict, and keep a running record of how the alerts turned out.

      Viva question bank

      Questions the professor is likely to ask, with model answers. Turn on quiz mode, answer out loud, then reveal.

      A. The basics

      A1What does your project do, in one sentence?

      After one cryptocurrency makes an unusually big hourly move, it predicts which other coins will make one of their own within 1, 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours, explains why, and shows it on a plain-language dashboard.

      A2What exactly is a "shock"?

      An hour in which a coin's time-of-day-adjusted return is more than 2.29 times its typical hourly move over the previous 168 hours (|z| > 2.29), up or down. The threshold was set on training data so that about 4% of hours are shocks.

      A3What do you mean by contagion?

      A shock in coin i at hour t followed by a shock in another coin j in a strictly later hour, within k hours. Same-hour moves are excluded because they are a common reaction to the same news, not spreading.

      A4Why is this useful?

      Traders and funds can cut risk on the coins most likely to be hit next, exchanges can raise margins before forced selling spreads, and regulators can watch systemic risk. Classic spillover measures describe average connectedness; they do not say who is hit next and how soon.

      A5Why cryptocurrencies?

      They trade 24/7, so hourly data has no gaps for nights or weekends; there are many tightly linked coins; crashes are frequent; and Binance publishes free, checksum-verified hourly data. Contagion here moves real money within hours.

      A6What are T1, T2 and T3?

      T1, the main task: pairwise contagion, solved by the TGN. T2: will a single coin shock in the next hour (LSTM against simple rules). T3: a coin's future volatility at 1, 6 and 24 hours (LSTM against HAR-RV).

      A7Why don't you predict whether prices go up or down?

      Our analysis showed hourly returns barely relate to past returns, so direction is close to unpredictable, while the size of moves clusters strongly in time. We predict what has signal. Big moves in either direction matter for risk.

      A8What is your contribution?

      A leakage-controlled hourly contagion benchmark (30 coins, 1.46 million events); a fair comparison of temporal graph models with network-science, econometric and machine-learning baselines (five seeds, day-block bootstrap); a TempME port and integrated-gradient analysis showing where the signal lives; persistence, robustness and anomaly analyses including negative results; and a plain-language dashboard with past-crisis replays.

      B. Data

      B1Where does the data come from?

      The Binance public archive: hourly bars for 30 coins, January 2020 to 27 September 2026. Every file was verified against its SHA-256 checksum (3,049 files, no failures). A separate daily dataset (Yahoo Finance and Coin Metrics) is used only for cross-checking.

      B2Why hourly data?

      Contagion in crypto happens within hours, and daily bars merge several shocks and their order into one observation; that was also your review comment on our first version. We tested 2-hour bars and 1 hour was better. Minute data is far bigger and noisier, and is listed as future work.

      B3How did you choose the 30 coins?

      From the largest coins by market value (CoinGecko, 27 September 2026) we kept the first 30 that are not stablecoins, wrapped or real-world-asset tokens, trade against USDT on Binance, have at least 730 days of hourly history and at least 90% coverage in the test period. Every exclusion is recorded with its reason.

      B4How big is the dataset?

      59,088 hours per coin, about 1.6 million coin-hours, and 1.46 million shock events: 360,590 for training, 300,998 for validation and 801,559 for test.

      B5How did you handle missing hours?

      Gaps of 1 or 2 hours are filled with the last price and flagged; longer gaps are left empty and the coin counts as inactive there. Nothing is filled before a coin was listed, and returns are never clipped.

      B6What went wrong with LUNA?

      Binance reused the ticker LUNAUSDT for a brand-new coin after the old Terra LUNA collapsed in May 2022. Joining the two would create a fake price jump, so they are never joined; the old coin is kept as LUNC, an event coin for case studies.

      B7What does the model know about each coin?

      14 numbers per hour: 6 market features (last return, volatility over 24 and 72 hours, de-seasonalised volume, volume against its past week, time since listing) and 8 network features updated daily (strength, eigenvector centrality, MST degree, community size, spillover PageRank and NET, shock PageRank, shock out-lift). Each event adds 5 numbers.

      B8How did you scale the features?

      Robust scaling per coin, using the median and interquartile range from the training years only, clipped at ±5. Volume and volatility are first divided by an hour-of-week profile fitted on training data, because activity follows the clock (0.69 to 2.17 times its average).

      B9Why check the USDT peg?

      Prices are quoted in USDT. If USDT itself lost its one-dollar value, every coin would appear to move together. We flag days when it was more than 0.5% off its peg, using Coin Metrics data until that archive ends in May 2026.

      C. Labels, splits and leakage

      C1What is data leakage, and how did you prevent it?

      Leakage is future information slipping into the inputs, which gives great but fake results. We use trailing windows only; fit every scaler and threshold on training data only; split by dates with a 7-day embargo; drop samples whose windows cross a split; update the TGN's memory only after a batch is predicted, with batches that never split an hour; and run automated tests on these rules.

      C2Why not a random train/test split?

      Shuffling time would let the model learn from the future and be tested on the past. In time series you train on earlier years and test on later ones.

      C3Why a 7-day embargo?

      Features use windows of up to a week, and market conditions carry over between neighbouring days. A gap at the start of validation and test stops the end of one period from informing the start of the next.

      C4Why the threshold 2.29?

      It is the level at which about 4% of training hours are shocks, comparable with the 3.9% rate of our daily data. It was fitted on training data only, so the test period could not influence it.

      C5Why the windows 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 hours?

      They run from immediate spread to a full day. Shocks typically calm down in 8 to 10 hours, so 24 hours covers a whole episode, and several windows show where memory starts to help.

      C6How imbalanced is the task?

      On the test period the share of "yes" answers is 10.5% within 1 hour, 20.6% within 3, 31.0% within 6, 44.7% within 12 and 62.7% within 24. That is why we use focal loss and AUPRC.

      C7Does the label depend on which coin shocked first?

      No: the answer for coin j depends only on j and the hour, so if several coins shock at once, j gets several events with the same answer. The model effectively asks which coins move next after a shock somewhere, and the dashboard shows one chance per coin. A direction-aware label is future work.

      D. The TGN

      D1Which model did you use?

      A Temporal Graph Network, TGN. Our version, ContagionTGN, uses PyTorch Geometric's TGN memory module, a graph-transformer attention layer over each coin's recent events, and our own five-output prediction head.

      D2Who proposed the TGN, and when?

      Emanuele Rossi, Ben Chamberlain, Fabrizio Frasca, Davide Eynard, Federico Monti and Michael Bronstein, researchers at Twitter with Imperial College London: "Temporal Graph Networks for Deep Learning on Dynamic Graphs", first published in June 2020 and presented at an ICML 2020 workshop.

      D3Explain the TGN simply.

      Every coin keeps a 64-number memory, like a diary. When a coin shocks, the model reads the diaries of that coin and each other coin, uses attention over each one's 10 most recent events, adds both coins' current features and the event's details, and outputs five chances. Only after the whole hour is scored does it write the hour's events into the diaries.

      D4How many parameters does it have?

      70,021 trainable parameters: 41,280 in the GRU that updates memory (59%), 17,984 in the attention layer (26%), 10,693 in the prediction head (15%) and 64 in two time encoders. The memory itself (30 × 64 numbers) is state, not parameters.

      D5Why so small?

      30 nodes and about 360 thousand training events. A small model overfits less and trains on a laptop CPU. A smaller 32-number variant did worse on validation, and stronger regularisation did not help either.

      D6How is the memory updated?

      Each event creates a message for both coins: their two memories, the event's 5 numbers and a 16-number time code (149 numbers). The latest message per coin goes through a GRU, which decides what to keep and what to overwrite, giving the new 64-number memory. This happens after the batch is predicted.

      D7What does the attention layer do?

      It is a graph-transformer layer (TransformerConv) with 2 heads of 32. For each coin it weighs its 10 most recent events, using their timing and messages, with weights that add up to 1, and produces a 64-number embedding.

      D8What goes into the prediction head?

      161 numbers: both embeddings (64 + 64), both coins' features at hour t (14 + 14) and the event message (5). A dense layer to 64 with ReLU and 20% dropout, then a layer to 5 outputs, each passed through a sigmoid.

      D9Why five sigmoids and not a softmax?

      The windows are not mutually exclusive: "within 6 hours" includes "within 3 hours". Each window is its own yes/no question (a multi-label problem). When serving, we also force the chances to never decrease with the window.

      D10How does the TGN avoid seeing the future?

      Events are processed in time order. For each batch the model predicts using memory and neighbour lists built only from earlier batches, then updates them. A batch never splits an hour, so events in the same hour cannot see each other. Features at hour t use data up to t.

      D11Why did you choose the TGN?

      Our data is a stream of timed events, which is exactly what the TGN was built for. Its per-coin memory captures recent turbulence. It combines each coin's own state with its relationships. Its predict-then-update design matches our no-peeking rule. It is small, fast and well established, with a maintained implementation in PyTorch Geometric, and explainers such as TempME work on it.

      D12Why not a GCN, a GAT or a snapshot GNN?

      A GCN or GAT on a single graph ignores time. A snapshot GNN (one graph per day plus a GRU) loses the order of events within the day. We planned the snapshot model but dropped it, because the TGN, DyGFormer and boosting comparison already answers whether the graph adds value.

      D13Why not a large Transformer or a language model?

      A large model needs far more data than 30 coins provide and would overfit. DyGFormer is the Transformer-style dynamic-graph model, and we tested it. Language models work on text, not on numeric event streams.

      D14Does the memory help?

      Not within 1 hour: without memory the score is the same (−0.003, interval −0.014 to +0.010). It does help from 6 to 24 hours, by 0.010 to 0.013 AUPRC (p at most 0.025). Memory captures the slower, multi-hour spread.

      D15What were the training settings?

      Adam with learning rate 0.0005 and weight decay 0.0001; focal loss (γ 2, α 0.25); batches of about 200 events; up to 100 epochs with early stopping after 10 epochs without improvement in validation AUPRC at 1 hour; five seeds.

      D16How long did training take, and on what?

      About 30 to 50 minutes per seed on a laptop CPU, with no GPU; the best epochs were 45 to 98. A full pass over the 1.46 million events takes one to two minutes.

      D17What is focal loss and why use it?

      A loss from Lin and colleagues (Facebook AI, 2017) that down-weights easy, well-classified examples, so the rare positives get more attention. It was chosen over a class-weighted cross-entropy by validation AUPRC.

      D18What is a seed, and why five?

      The seed fixes the random starting values. Five trainings show how much of a result is luck: the spread is about ±0.005 AUPRC, and we report the average. Comparisons use the average of the five models' predictions.

      D19Why are there two final TGN versions?

      Version 1 (up to 30 epochs, patience 4) stopped while its validation curve was still rising (0.138). Looking only at validation curves, we raised the budget to 100 epochs with patience 10: version 2, validation 0.153, which is the main model. Both are reported.

      E. The other models

      E1Why did you choose these models only?

      Each one tests a claim. Random and simple rules show there is signal. The independent cascade is the network-science baseline. Gradient boosting tests whether plain machine learning on the same inputs is enough. The LSTM tests a single coin's history without the graph. DyGFormer checks that the result does not depend on one architecture. The no-memory TGN isolates what memory adds.

      E2What is the independent cascade model?

      An epidemic-style diffusion model (Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos, 2003). Each shocked coin gets one chance to "infect" each other coin, with a probability estimated from training shocks and shrunk towards that coin's base rate. With several shocked coins, the chance for j is one minus the product of all the "miss" chances.

      E3Why did gradient boosting lose?

      It matched the TGN on validation (0.155 against 0.153) but had memorised its training years (training score 0.64), so it generalised worse to the test period: 0.178 against 0.205 at 1 hour. From 6 hours on the two are tied.

      E4What is DyGFormer, and why re-implement it?

      A Transformer over each coin's last 16 events, cut into patches of 2, with an encoding of how often the two coins appeared together (Yu and colleagues, NeurIPS 2023). The official library trains with random fake links and splits time by fractions, which would break our leakage rules, so we wrote a compact version: 24,021 parameters. It ties the TGN.

      E5Why did the LSTM lose to HAR-RV on volatility?

      Volatility is smooth and well described by a simple linear formula of recent volatility (last hour, day and week). A neural network adds noise there. Deep learning earns its place on rare events and networks.

      E6What is T-GAE, and why did it fail?

      A temporal graph autoencoder that learns what normal hours look like and flags hours it cannot reproduce. Stress days are extreme market-wide moves that a simple turbulence measure already catches (AUROC 0.89 against 0.51). It is reported as a negative result, though it ranked 10 October 2025 as the top anomaly.

      E7What did you try that did not work?

      Futures-market data (validation fell to about 0.13 for the TGN and 0.140 for boosting), retraining the TGN on train plus validation (0.205 down to 0.188), 2-hour bars, a learning-rate schedule, extra context features, stronger regularisation and a smaller model. All are reported.

      F. Evaluation and results

      F1Why not accuracy?

      89.5% of one-hour events are "no", so a model that always says "no" is 89.5% accurate and useless. We use AUPRC, AUROC and the precision of the top 1% of alerts.

      F2What is AUPRC, and what does random score?

      Average precision over the ranked list of events: each time a real positive is reached, take the precision so far, then average. A perfect ranking scores 1; random scores the positive rate (0.105 within 1 hour). The TGN's 0.205 is about twice random.

      F3Why not F1 score?

      F1 needs one fixed threshold. AUPRC summarises every threshold and rewards a good ranking, which is what alerts need: the riskiest coins first.

      F4What AUROC did you get?

      0.64 to 0.69 across windows, where 0.5 is random. Clearly above chance but modest, because most shocks are news-driven and arrive across the market in the same hour.

      F5What is a day-block bootstrap?

      We rebuild the test period 1,000 times by drawing its 971 days with replacement, compute model A's AUPRC minus model B's each time, and take the middle 95% as the interval. Whole days, because one crash creates hundreds of dependent events; resampling single events would make us overconfident.

      F6What are your main results?

      Test AUPRC for the TGN: 0.205, 0.352, 0.464, 0.588 and 0.739 at 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 hours, against random 0.105 to 0.627 and the independent cascade 0.159 to 0.672. It beats the cascade at every window, beats boosting at 1 to 3 hours (borderline), ties DyGFormer, and memory helps at 6 to 24 hours.

      F7Is the TGN significantly better than the cascade?

      Yes, at every window: +0.046 at 1 hour (interval 0.019 to 0.074), +0.084 at 6 hours, and no bootstrap resample favoured the cascade.

      F8Why does DyGFormer tie the TGN?

      Both pick up the same signal: the receiving coin's turbulence and the market's breadth. Two different designs agreeing makes the result more credible, not less.

      F9Where does the graph help most?

      After market-wide crashes (TGN 0.245 against boosting 0.205 at 1 hour). After isolated shocks they tie (0.114 against 0.117): when one coin moves on its own news, the network adds nothing.

      F10How good are the top alerts?

      Of the 1% riskiest test events, 32% came true within 1 hour (random 10.5%), 61% within 3, 76% within 6 and 89% within 24 (random 63%).

      F11Did a simple model do as well?

      In our final review, a four-feature logistic regression (the coin's volume against its past week, hours since its last shock, market breadth over the last day, size of its current move) matched the TGN on the test period. It confirms the explanations: at hourly resolution most of the signal is the receiving coin's own turbulence. The TGN learns this from the raw stream and clearly beats the network baseline; finer data is where a graph model could pull ahead.

      F12How many times did you use the test set?

      Each final configuration scored it once, and every one is listed in the report's Table VI: TGN version 1 and 2, three no-memory versions, DyGFormer, boosting, the two refits and the 2-hour TGN. The main model was fixed from validation evidence and never switched for a better test score.

      F13Would this make money?

      We have not tested that. A money-based backtest (for example stepping out of flagged coins for 6 hours and measuring losses avoided) is future work. The project predicts risk, not direction.

      G. Explanations and robustness

      G1How do you explain a prediction?

      With integrated gradients on the prediction head: how much each input pushed the score up or down compared with an average situation. The pushes add up exactly to the score, which we check every run. TempME was ported to look for chains of past events.

      G2What do the explanations show?

      About 65% of the attribution comes from the receiving coin's own state (volume against its past week 25%, 24-hour volatility 12%, last return 9%), about 25% from the source coin, 10% from the event, and memory about 7%. The model flags coins that are already stretched when another coin crashes.

      G3Why was TempME weak?

      Its fidelity was barely above random (0.157 against 0.152) and below taking the most recent events (0.192). The TGN's attention is almost uniform and shocks spread across the market in the same hour, so there are no chains of events to find. It is a finding about the market.

      G4How robust is the model?

      Removing up to half of the past events changes the score by under 0.5%; random noise of 0.5 standard deviations costs 5.7%. A targeted FGSM attack of only 0.1 standard deviations costs about 35%, while random noise of the same size costs under 1%. The risk is deliberate manipulation, not noise.

      G5What is FGSM?

      The fast gradient sign method (Goodfellow and colleagues, 2015): nudge every input a small step in the direction that increases the model's error the most. We also ran PGD, a 10-step version, which was barely stronger, so FGSM is close to the worst case here.

      H1What does the dashboard show?

      Five tabs. Who's at risk: every coin's chance of a big move within the chosen window, against a typical coin. Why: the inputs behind one coin's risk, in plain words. Past crises: nine catalogued crises with the model's top picks against random picks and an hour-by-hour replay. Market map: coins grouped by sector and linked by how closely they moved. How it works: plain explanations, the model's test scores and how long big moves last. Above the tabs, "What just happened" describes the newest big moves.

      H2How is a chance on the page calculated?

      The TGN's raw score is calibrated by binned isotonic regression fitted on validation predictions (never the test years), made non-decreasing across windows, and for each coin the highest value over the events pointing at it is shown.

      H3What does "× typical coin" mean?

      The lift: the coin's chance divided by the base rate, how often any coin moved after a shock in the validation years. 1.5 or more is shown as "much higher than usual", 1.15 or more as "higher than usual".

      H4Is it real time?

      No. It shows the newest prediction on the research data, which ends on 27 September 2026, plus nine crisis replays. Refreshing it means running the data pipeline on new Binance hours and then cryptograph-predict; the model does not change. Automatic hourly updates are future work.

      H5Does the page run the model when I open it?

      No. cryptograph-predict replays the whole event stream through the trained TGN, scores the newest hour with big moves and saves the chances and reasons as small files. The page only reads and draws them, so it opens fast and nothing is retrained.

      H6Why does the dashboard show 0.20 when the report says 0.205?

      The dashboard serves one trained model (seed 0), whose test AUPRC at 1 hour is 0.203. The report's 0.205 is the average of five seeds.

      H7Why Streamlit?

      It turns a Python script into an interactive web page with no separate web stack, works directly with our pandas and Plotly code, and suits a research team.

      H8Why do most crisis replays look no better than random?

      In a market-wide crash nearly every coin moves, so any pick is right; the page says so itself. The bitcoin ETF approval (January 2024, test period) is where the model clearly beat random picks: 3 of its 5 riskiest coins moved within 6 hours, against 1.6 for random picks.

      J1What is in each of your six notebooks?

      J3Why does notebook 01 have a daily part and an hourly part?

      The project started on ten years of daily prices (Yahoo Finance, cross-checked with Coin Metrics). After the review we moved the models to hourly Binance data, because contagion happens within hours: a shock makes another coin's shock 2.3 times more likely in the very next hour. Part I keeps the basic facts on the longest history; Part II covers the data the models actually use.

      J4How did you check data quality?

      Daily: nine checks on every coin-day; 28 bad prices corrected; 123 of 156 moves above 40% confirmed by a second source or a known crisis; missing days left empty, never invented. Hourly: 3,049 files verified by SHA-256; maintenance gaps of up to 2 hours filled and flagged (13 hours), longer ones masked; ticker changes handled; daily totals match the daily dataset to about 0.1%; four USDT off-peg days flagged.

      J5How did you handle MATIC to POL and the LUNA collapse?

      MATIC to POL was a 1:1 swap: a 79-hour gap, no price jump, so the gap is masked and the series continues. LUNA: the LUNAUSDT name was reused for a new coin after the May 2022 collapse, so the old coin (now LUNC) and the new LUNA are never stitched together; the 2,863-hour gap stays masked. FTT's 7,467-hour gap is its delisting after FTX.

      J6Why do you never feed prices to the model?

      Prices drift and grow, so patterns found in them are often fake. The ADF test could not reject "wandering" for 15 of 20 coins' log prices, while returns were stationary for all 20. So all inputs are built from returns and volatility.

      J7Why predict shocks and not whether prices go up or down?

      Because direction is not predictable from the past: yesterday's direction correlates −0.024 with today's, and the sign repeats only 47% of days. Jumpiness is predictable: +0.184 for squared returns, and 55 to 65% of shocks are followed by another within a day. After a shock, the direction of the next move is also mostly unpredictable (notebook 05).

      J8Why remove intraday seasonality?

      Jumpiness follows the clock: Wednesday 12:00 UTC is 2.17 times an average hour, Sunday 06:00 is 0.69 times. Without the correction, ordinary Wednesday afternoons would be labelled shocks and Sunday mornings never. The profile is fitted on training data and divided out before features and shocks are computed.

      J9Does Bitcoin lead the market?

      No. Coins move together in the same hour (correlation 0.65 with BTC), but one hour later the link is tiny (0.035 at most, and that peak is a 24-hour daily rhythm). After correction only 8 of 45 BTC/ETH-to-coin Granger tests pass, the effects are about 0.02% of variance, and the pattern does not repeat on validation. So the graph has no "who leads" arrows; direction comes from which coin shocked first.

      J10How did you choose the graph threshold of 0.75?

      It was fitted on training data to give the same density as the daily graph, about 30% of possible links. With week-long windows almost every pair clears 0.5 (75% of pairs), a hairball with no structure. 0.75 at 1 hour, 0.77 at 2 hours, plus each coin's two strongest links so no coin is alone.

      J11What does the eigenvalue of 19.3 mean?

      The 30 eigenvalues of the correlation table add up to 30; the biggest, 19.3, says one common direction, "the whole market moving", explains 64% of hourly movement. It is the only one above the 1.08 that pure noise could produce. Most of a coin's hourly move is the market.

      J12Your hourly spillover ranking equals eigenvector centrality. Is that a problem?

      It is a finding. With one lag and tiny lagged effects, the hourly decomposition is driven by same-hour correlation, so NET measures how tied a coin is to the market, not who moves first. That is why we did not treat it as ground truth: explanations were also compared with the daily ranking and with the time-ordered shock-transmission PageRank.

      J13Do sectors matter?

      For grouping, partly: communities match sectors with NMI 0.47 against 0.32 for shuffled labels (p = 0.003). For contagion, no: another coin shocks within the hour equally often inside and across sectors (9.3% and 9.3%), except meme coins (13.7% against 10.9%). So the sector is a coin feature, not the shape of the graph.

      J14What does the minimum spanning tree show? Why is DOT the hub?

      It is the market's skeleton: the 29 strongest links joining all 30 coins. DOGE–SHIB and BTC–ETH are the strongest; ETH is the hub of the big coins. DOT has the most links (11), but to loners such as TRX, ZEC and QNT whose nearest neighbour happens to be DOT, so it is a meeting point for outsiders, not a leader. The tree shrinks in every crisis.

      J15Walk me through FTX hour by hour.

      6 November 15:00 UTC: Binance's CEO says he will sell FTT; FTT falls 14 standard deviations and no other coin shocks. 8 November 02:00: FTT falls 10 and 18 of 30 coins shock in the same hour; 16:00, the takeover news, 20 coins jump together; 17:00 to 18:00, as the deal unravels, FTT falls 17 and all 24 coins trading then shock down. A random hour has about 1.2. Contagion was simultaneous and news-driven, not a chain.

      J16Why is test AUPRC (0.205) higher than validation (0.153)?

      The test years (2024 to 2026) had more shocks and more moving together than 2022 to 2023: the positive rate is 10.5% against 8.6% at 1 hour, and even the cascade scores higher on test. Compared with random, the gap is smaller: lift 1.95 on test against 1.78 on validation. It is a property of the period, not a sign of leakage.

      J17Why do the tables count 795,787 test events, not 801,559?

      The stream holds 801,559 test events; only events after the 7-day embargo and with their whole answer window inside the test period are scored. That leaves 795,787 at 1 hour and slightly fewer at 12 and 24 hours, whose windows run past 27 September 2026 at the very end.

      J18Is your TGN overfitting?

      Somewhat, at short windows. Its lift drops from training to validation by a ratio of about 1.5 at 1 hour, against about 1.1 for the cascade, which cannot memorise. At 24 hours all models are close to the cascade (1.08 to 1.14). Stronger regularisation or a smaller model narrowed the gap only by losing validation accuracy, so early stopping keeps it under control.

      J19Did you train on a GPU?

      No. Every final run logged a CPU-only PyTorch build, as the report says. A TGN epoch over the 360,590 training events took about half a minute. Notebook 01 mentions a Colab GPU as the original plan for the full stream, but the CPU was enough.

      J20Why is the TGN's attention perfectly uniform? Is that a bug?

      No. The captured inputs reproduce the model to 2.4 × 10−7, removing half of the past events changes the score by under 0.5%, and the no-memory model is as good at 1 hour. The model learned that no single recent event matters more than the others, which fits the market: shocks hit many coins in the same hour, so event patterns look the same as in a time-shuffled stream.

      J21What did notebook 05 find, and what does it mean for the model?

      Shocks fade fast: a coin is back to normal after a median of 8 to 10 hours, and the extra jumpiness against an ordinary hour halves in about 2.5 hours. 55 to 65% re-shock within a day; market-wide crashes pull in about 8 other coins within 6 hours against about 5 for isolated shocks. So the 1 to 24-hour labels cover a shock's whole life, and the TGN helps most after broad shocks.

      J22Which days did the T-GAE flag?

      Its top test day is 10 October 2025, the liquidation cascade. The next nine cluster in early 2024 and mid 2026; most were not stress days and none is near a known event, so we report them as "unclassified": the score says a day's structure was unusual, not why, and we claim no manipulation.

      J23What is in notebooks/archive, and why keep it?

      Our first two draft notebooks, kept unchanged with a README listing every error. Three examples: the shock was measured against a window that included the same day (leakage); "38.71% contagion" counted coins shocking on the same day, which is co-occurrence, not contagion; and the spillover numbers came from the order-dependent Cholesky method, which made Bitcoin look like a giant.       K. Techniques and statistics

      K1What is a p-value, in plain words?

      How surprising the data would be if nothing real were going on. A small p-value means "hard to explain by luck". It is not the probability that our claim is true.

      K2What is the null hypothesis of the ADF test?

      That the series has a unit root: it wanders and never returns to a stable level. Rejecting it (a small p-value) means the series is stationary.

      K3What does an excess kurtosis of 18 mean?

      Extreme moves are far more common than a bell curve allows (a bell curve has 0). A squared-error loss would be dominated by a few crash days, so we use Huber or focal loss, clip inputs, and score with AUPRC.

      K4Is Granger causality real causality?

      No. It only asks whether one series' past improves forecasts of another beyond its own past. A common cause, or a coin whose price updates late (thin trading, as with QNT), can produce it without any real influence.

      K5Why Benjamini–Hochberg rather than Bonferroni?

      With 90 tests, Bonferroni guards against even one fluke and would hide real effects. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the share of flukes among the results called significant (about 5%), the right balance for a screening question like "is there any lead-lag?".

      K6Why a circular shift rather than a random shuffle for the lead-lag null?

      A shuffle destroys each coin's own calm and stormy spells, which makes the null too easy to beat. Rotating the series keeps those habits and only breaks the timing link. It also keeps the 24-hour rhythm, so it is an upper limit, which is why Granger tests were run as well.

      K7What is the Marchenko–Pastur bound?

      The biggest eigenvalue that pure noise could produce for a given number of coins and hours, (1 + √(N/T))², here 1.08. Eigenvalues above it are real structure; only the market factor (19.3) is.

      K8Generalized against Cholesky decomposition: what is the difference?

      Cholesky assumes the first variable can affect the others within the same period but not the reverse, so results depend on the order of the coins. The generalized version (Pesaran and Shin, 1998) is order-free. Our first draft used Cholesky and Bitcoin, listed first, looked like a giant transmitter.

      K9What does Louvain optimise, and why remove the market mode first?

      Modularity: links inside groups minus what chance would give. Without removing the market factor every coin is strongly tied to every other and Louvain finds one big group.

      K10Why the distance √(2(1 − ρ)) for the MST?

      It turns a correlation into a proper distance (Mantegna, 1999): 0 for identical coins, 1.41 for unrelated ones, 2 for opposites, and it obeys the triangle rule, which a tree algorithm needs.

      K11Why is PageRank run on a "depends on" network?

      With the arrow pointing from the driven coin to the driving coin, importance flows to the coins that others depend on. With the arrows the other way it would collect at the receivers.

      K12What is the "shrinkage" in your independent cascade?

      Each pair's chance is pulled towards the receiver's normal shock rate, as if we had 10 extra observations at that rate: (hits + 10 × base rate) ÷ (tries + 10). Pairs with little data get sensible numbers instead of 0% or 100%.

      K13What is a GCN, and where did you use one?

      A graph convolution: each node takes a weighted average of its neighbours' numbers and its own, then applies a learned transformation. Two GCN layers are the first part of the T-GAE.

      K14In integrated gradients, what is the baseline and what is completeness?

      The baseline is the "average situation", the training mean of every input. Completeness means the attributions add up exactly to the score at the real input minus the score at the baseline; we check it every run and the largest gap was 0.009.

      K15What is the characterization score?

      One number for how faithful an event explanation is: it combines "the chosen events alone keep the prediction" and "removing them changes it" (a harmonic mean of fidelity+ and 1 − fidelity−). 1 would be perfect; TempME reached 0.157 against 0.152 for random choice.

      K16FGSM against PGD?

      FGSM takes one step of size ε in the most harmful direction. PGD takes 10 smaller steps from a random start, staying within ε, so it should find worse inputs. Here it was within 0.002 of FGSM, which means the model behaves almost like a straight line at these sizes and FGSM was already near the worst case.

      K17Why was int8 quantisation slower, not faster?

      Most of the time goes into looking up each coin's recent events and updating memories, not into multiplying numbers; int8 only speeds up the multiplications and adds conversion work. So it is 3.1 times smaller but 1.8 times slower, and we serve the full model (about 6 microseconds per event).

      K18Spearman or Pearson: when do you use which?

      Pearson for returns, where the values matter. Spearman, which uses only the order, for comparing rankings (spillover, PageRank, explanations) and for checking redundant features, because it ignores extreme values and scale.

      K19Why rank-average models in the ensemble?

      Different models' chances are not on the same scale (boosting's scores are spread differently from the TGN's). Ranks put them on one scale; the weights are then chosen per window on validation only.

      K20What exactly is HAR-RV?

      A straight-line formula (Corsi, 2009): future volatility = a + b × last hour's + c × last day's + d × last week's volatility, fitted by least squares on training data. It mixes short and long memory, which is why it is so hard to beat.

      L. The report

      L1What are your research questions, and what did you find?

      RQ1, do temporal graph models beat a fitted diffusion model? Yes, at every window. RQ2, do they beat plain machine learning, and does memory matter? Only borderline against boosting at 1 to 3 hours; memory helps only from 6 hours. RQ3, what drives alerts? The receiving coin's current state. RQ4, how long do shocks last and how robust is the model? About half a day; robust to noise and gaps, vulnerable to deliberate attacks.

      L2Explain equation 4.

      It builds the message that updates coin i's memory: its memory just before the event (64 numbers), the other coin's memory (64), the event's 5 numbers and a 16-number code of the time since i was last updated, glued together (149 numbers). Equation 5 feeds it to a GRU, which writes the new memory.

      L3Explain equation 3. Why "t + 1"?

      The label is 1 if coin j has at least one shock in the hours t + 1 to t + k. Starting at t + 1 means the same hour never counts: a same-hour label would call simultaneous co-movement contagion, which the report explicitly rejects.

      L4What did Forbes and Rigobon argue, and how do you respect it?

      That much apparent contagion is just normal interdependence, which looks bigger in volatile times. We define shocks relative to each coin's own recent volatility and only count shocks that come strictly later.

      L5What gap does your work fill?

      Finance studies measure how connected coins are; graph-learning studies test models on "will these two interact?". Nobody had a fair hourly test for pairwise contagion, a fair race between temporal graph models and finance, network and machine-learning baselines, and an analysis of what the models learn.

      L6How did you choose the 30 coins? Why is XMR missing?

      The first 30 in CoinGecko's market-cap ranking of 27 September 2026 (top 60, no stablecoins or wrapped tokens) with a Binance USDT market, at least 730 days of hourly history and at least 90% test coverage. XMR was delisted by Binance in February 2024. Every exclusion is recorded. The weak spot is survivorship bias, which the report admits.

      L7Why did you not use the official DyGFormer library?

      It draws random "negative" examples for link prediction and splits data by fractions, both of which would break our rules (fixed dates, an embargo, the same real events for every model). So we rebuilt a compact version (24,021 parameters) on exactly our events.

      L8What does "memory is updated only after a batch has been scored" mean, and why do batches never split an hour?

      The model must answer before it learns from that hour's events; updating first would let it peek at the answers. Keeping each hour in one batch means no event can benefit from another event of the same hour.

      L9The event vector has a "shock flag". Isn't it always 1?

      Yes: every event in our stream starts with a shock, so the flag is constant and carries no information. It marks the event type, so the same format could take other kinds of events later; it does no harm. The four informative numbers are the two correlations, the source's return and its volatility.

      L10Explain Table VI. Why list versions you did not use?

      It is the full list of everything we scored on the test period, including superseded versions (TGN v1 0.201, no-memory v1 and v2) and rejected ones (TGN refit 0.188). Looking at the test several times risks the "garden of forking paths", quietly keeping whatever scored best. Listing everything, and fixing the main model from validation evidence, makes that visible and checkable.

      L11The no-memory model scored 0.206, 0.195 and 0.208 in three versions. What does that tell you?

      That differences of about 0.01 can come from training choices alone (here, version 2 stopped on a noisy early peak, fixed by a minimum of 30 epochs). It is why we use five seeds, a bootstrap, and call the boosting comparison borderline.

      L12Why is accuracy "meaningless" here, and why AUPRC instead of AUROC?

      Always saying "no" is 89.5% accurate at 1 hour and catches nothing. With rare positives, AUROC can look good while the alerts are mostly false; precision–recall curves are more informative (Davis and Goadrich, 2006), and AUPRC's random level is simply the positive rate.

      L13Why do bootstrap tests use five-seed ensembles while tables report seed means?

      A test compares one prediction per event for each model, so the five seeds are averaged into one ensemble prediction. Tables report the mean score over seeds, the usual way to describe a model's typical performance. The two differ by about 0.001.

      L14What do Figures 4 and 5 show?

      Figure 4(a): each model's AUPRC divided by the positive rate, per window, so windows with different base rates can be compared; 4(b): precision of the riskiest 1% against random. Figure 5: the TGN minus each of four rivals with 95% bootstrap intervals; a filled marker means the interval excludes zero.

      L15The report says the TGN's edge is "in overall ranking, not in the very top". Explain.

      Across all events the TGN ranks real contagion higher than boosting. But among the top 1% most confident alerts, DyGFormer (43% at 1 hour) and a refitted boosting model (34%) are as precise or more precise than the TGN (32%).

      L16Why call the 13 of 15 crash alerts "an anecdote, not a statistic"?

      Fifteen alerts are too few for a reliable rate, and two of the three crashes (LUNA, FTX) lie in the training and validation years, which the model had seen or was tuned on. Only October 2025 is a true test case.

      L17What is survivorship bias in your study?

      We chose coins from a 2026 ranking, so coins that collapsed or were delisted before then are missing. The market may look calmer and more connected than it really was for an investor in 2020.

      L18The report says calibration was not checked on the test period. Did you calibrate at all?

      Yes, for the dashboard: chances are calibrated on validation data (binned isotonic, at least 500 events per bin, rising with the window). What the report admits is that we did not measure on the test period how well those chances match reality.

      L19Why monitor the USDT peg? (reference 15)

      All Binance prices are quoted in USDT, so if USDT itself slips from $1, every price moves for a reason that has nothing to do with the coins. And the USDT premium is itself a crisis signal (Vo, 2026). We flagged the four off-peg days rather than removing them.

      L20What is CryptoGAT, and how is your work different?

      A June 2026 paper that predicts crypto prices with graph attention across coins and questions purely time-series models. It predicts prices; we predict pairwise shock contagion hour by hour, with a temporal graph model and a no-peeking event stream.

      L21What is TGAT, and how does it relate to the TGN?

      TGAT (Xu and colleagues, ICLR 2020) applies attention over a node's time-stamped neighbours with a learned time encoding. The TGN reuses that attention and time encoding and adds a memory per node updated by a GRU.

      L22What do "197 passed and 5 skipped" and "within 0.0007" mean?

      At submission, the automated test suite ran 197 checks successfully and skipped 5 (integration tests that only run when the full built dataset is on the machine). Replaying the saved final TGN on the test events reproduced its stored AUPRC to within 0.0007, so the reported number can be regenerated. (The deployed branch has 210 tests now.)

      L23Table I says 1,463,693 events but your splits add up to 1,463,147. Why?

      The first number was counted while the data were built; the stream skips the 546 shocks in the first week of January 2020, because each event's correlation numbers need a full week of history. Both are "about 1.46 million".

      L24Why did you cite so many 2026 Digital Finance papers?

      To show the gap in current work: recent studies (Kammoun; Nefzi and colleagues; Zarifian and colleagues; Vo; Mohamad; Teruel-Gutiérrez and Aparicio Serrano; Alamsyah and colleagues) still measure spillovers, connectedness or event responses, some with hourly data, but none predicts for each pair of coins whether a shock will be followed by another within hours.

      Glossary in plain words

      Every technical word in this guide, explained in one or two sentences.

      Active mask
      1 when a coin exists and has data that hour, 0 otherwise; the models ignore the zeros.
      Adam
      The rule used to adjust the knobs during training; it picks a step size for each knob on its own.
      ADF test
      Tests whether a series wanders off for good (a unit root) or keeps returning to a stable level.
      Assortativity
      Whether nodes link mostly to their own kind (positive) or to others (negative).
      Attention
      Giving different weights to different inputs (adding up to 1), so the important ones count more.
      AUPRC (average precision)
      How well a model ranks the real positives at the top; random scores the positive rate.
      AUROC
      The chance a real positive is ranked above a real negative; 0.5 is random, 1 is perfect.
      Autocorrelation (ACF)
      How much a series looks like itself a few steps earlier.
      Autoencoder
      A network trained to rebuild its input; what it rebuilds badly is unusual.
      Base rate (positive rate)
      How often the answer is "yes"; the score of random guessing.
      Batch
      A group of examples processed together; about 200 events for the TGN.
      Benjamini–Hochberg
      A correction for running many tests, so that few of the results called significant are flukes.
      Beta
      How strongly a coin swings when the market swings.
      BIC
      A score for choosing a model's size: rewards a good fit, charges for every extra number.
      Bootstrap (day-block)
      Rebuilding the test period many times from randomly drawn whole days, to see how much a result could be luck.
      Breadth (of a shock)
      The share of other coins shocking in the same hour: market-wide (30% or more), partial, or isolated (under 10%).
      Calibration
      Making scores mean real chances: a 30% risk should happen about 30% of the time.
      Candle (OHLCV)
      One hour of trading in five numbers: open, high, low, close and volume.
      Characterization score
      One number for how faithful an event explanation is; combines fidelity+ and fidelity−.
      Checksum (SHA-256)
      A fingerprint of a file; any change to the file changes the fingerprint.
      Circular-shift null
      Fake data made by rotating a series in time, keeping its habits but breaking any timing link.
      Confidence interval
      A range of believable values for the true answer; if it excludes 0, a difference is reliable.
      Contagion
      A shock in one coin followed by shocks in other coins within hours.
      Correlation
      How closely two series move together, from −1 to +1.
      Data leakage
      Future information sneaking into a model's inputs, giving fake good results.
      Diebold-Yilmaz spillover
      A finance method that measures how much of each asset's volatility comes from the others.
      Diversification
      Spreading money over many coins so one bad coin hurts less; fails when everything falls together.
      Dropout
      Switching off random neurons during training so the network does not lean on any one of them.
      DyGFormer
      A Transformer model for event streams (NeurIPS 2023); our second-opinion model.
      Early stopping
      Stopping training when the validation score stops improving, and keeping the best version.
      Eigenvalue (market mode)
      How much of all the movement one common direction explains; the biggest is “the whole market” (64%).
      Embargo
      A gap (7 days) between the training, validation and test periods.
      Embedding
      A short list of numbers that summarises something complicated.
      Epoch
      One full pass over the training data.
      Epps effect
      Correlations looking weaker when measured over very short intervals; weak in crypto.
      ETF
      A fund traded on the stock market that holds an asset, such as Bitcoin since January 2024.
      Event
      "Coin i shocked at hour t; what about coin j?", one per other coin per shock.
      Fat tails (kurtosis)
      Extreme moves far more common than a bell curve allows; kurtosis measures it (18 for crypto, 0 for a bell curve).
      FGSM
      A deliberate attack that nudges inputs in the direction that hurts the model most.
      Focal loss
      A loss that focuses training on rare, hard examples.
      Fresh shock
      A shock with no shock of the same coin in the previous 24 hours.
      Funding rate
      The small fee traders of perpetual futures pay each other to keep the futures price near the real price.
      GCN
      Graph convolution: each node averages its neighbours' numbers, then applies a learned transformation.
      GFEVD
      Generalized forecast-error variance decomposition: splits a coin's forecast uncertainty into shares from each coin, order-free.
      GNN
      A graph neural network: each node updates itself by listening to its neighbours.
      Gradient boosting
      Many small decision trees added one after another, each fixing the last ones' mistakes.
      Granger causality
      Whether one series' past improves forecasts of another beyond its own past; prediction, not proof of cause.
      Graph
      Dots (nodes) joined by lines (edges); here, coins and their relationships.
      GRU
      A small memory cell with gates that decide what to keep and what to overwrite.
      Half-life
      The time until an extra effect has halved; about 2.5 hours for a shock's extra jumpiness.
      HAR-RV
      A simple formula that forecasts volatility from the last hour, day and week.
      Horizon (window)
      How far ahead we look: 1, 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours.
      Huber loss
      Squares small errors but counts big ones linearly, so a few crash days cannot dominate training.
      Independent cascade
      An epidemic-style model: each shocked coin gets one chance to infect each other coin.
      Information bottleneck
      Explaining with as little as possible: keep the prediction while keeping few inputs.
      Integrated gradients
      An explanation method that splits a prediction into each input's push up or down.
      Interdependence
      Coins always moving together anyway, which is not the same as contagion (Forbes and Rigobon).
      Isotonic regression
      Fitting an always-rising curve; used to turn scores into calibrated chances.
      Jaccard similarity
      Overlap of two lists: shared items divided by all items.
      Jarque–Bera test
      Tests whether returns could come from a bell curve, using their lopsidedness and tail weight.
      Lead-lag
      One series moving first and another following later; almost absent between coins at hourly speed.
      Leverage
      Trading with borrowed money, which multiplies both gains and losses.
      Lift
      A score divided by the random score; "× typical coin" on the dashboard.
      Liquidation cascade
      Forced selling of borrowed-money positions pushing prices down and forcing more selling, like dominoes.
      Liquidity
      How easily a coin trades without moving its price; thinly traded coins update late.
      Logistic regression
      One weighted sum of the inputs squeezed into a probability; the simplest learned classifier.
      Logit
      The raw score before the sigmoid; 0 means 50%.
      Log return
      ln(price now ÷ price before); almost the percentage change, and it adds up neatly over time.
      Louvain
      An algorithm that finds groups (communities) in a network by maximising modularity.
      LSTM
      A neural network with memory that reads a sequence, such as one coin's past week.
      Marchenko–Pastur bound
      The biggest eigenvalue pure noise would give; anything above it is real structure (1.08 here).
      Market capitalisation
      Price × number of coins in existence: a coin's total size.
      Memory (TGN)
      A 64-number diary per coin, updated after each hour's events.
      Message
      The 5 numbers describing an event, used to update memory and to predict.
      Modularity
      How cleanly a network splits into groups; 0 means no group structure.
      Motif (temporal)
      A small pattern of events in time order, such as a → b then b → c.
      MST (minimum spanning tree)
      The skeleton of the strongest links that connects all coins without loops.
      NMI
      Normalised mutual information: how much two groupings agree, from 0 to 1.
      Null hypothesis
      The boring starting assumption of a test, such as “there is no effect”.
      Overfitting
      Memorising the training data instead of learning patterns that carry over.
      PageRank
      Google's importance score for web pages, used here for how important a coin is as a spreader.
      Parameter
      One adjustable knob in a model, learned during training. The TGN has 70,021.
      PGD
      A stronger, step-by-step version of the FGSM attack (10 small steps).
      Precision / recall
      Of the alarms, how many were real / of the real events, how many raised an alarm.
      p-value
      How surprising the data would be if the null hypothesis were true; small means hard to explain by luck.
      PyTorch / PyTorch Geometric
      The deep-learning library we use, and its add-on for graph models.
      Quantisation (int8)
      Storing a model's numbers as 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit decimals, to make it smaller.
      R²
      The share of the ups and downs a formula explains, from 0 to 1.
      ReLU
      A simple bend in a network: keep positive numbers, turn negatives into zero.
      Robust scaling
      Centre on the median and divide by the interquartile range, fitted on training data only.
      Seasonality (intraday)
      Regular patterns by hour of the week, such as busy US mornings and quiet Sunday mornings.
      Seed
      The number that fixes a training run's random starting point.
      Shock
      An hour with a move above 2.29 times the coin's typical hourly move, up or down.
      Sigmoid
      Squeezes any number into 0 to 1, read as a probability.
      Spearman correlation
      Correlation between rankings rather than raw values.
      Spillover index (TSI)
      The average share of each coin's forecast uncertainty that comes from other coins (91.6% hourly).
      Stablecoin
      A coin built to stay worth one US dollar, such as USDT or USDC.
      Standard deviation
      The typical distance from the average; for returns, it is the volatility.
      Stationary
      Behaving the same way over time: returns are, prices are not.
      Streamlit
      A Python library that turns a script into an interactive web page.
      Stress day
      A day with at least one hour in which 70% or more of active coins shocked.
      Survivorship bias
      Studying only today's survivors, which hides the coins that died along the way.
      Systemic risk
      The risk that trouble in one place brings down the whole system.
      TempME
      An explainer (NeurIPS 2023) that looks for chains of past events behind a prediction.
      Temporal graph
      A graph whose lines appear at specific times, so order matters.
      T-GAE
      Temporal graph autoencoder: learns what a normal day's network looks like and flags surprising days; 3,646 parameters.
      TGAT
      Temporal graph attention (2020): attention over time-stamped neighbours with a time code; the TGN reuses it.
      TGN
      Temporal Graph Network (Twitter, 2020): memory per node, attention over recent events, a prediction head.
      Transformer
      A model built on attention (2017); the basis of DyGFormer and of language models.
      Turbulence
      A day's average |z| across coins: how wild the market was.
      USDT
      A coin designed to stay worth one US dollar; Binance prices are quoted in it.
      Validation set
      The "mock exam" years used to choose settings, kept apart from the final test years.
      VAR
      Vector autoregression: predicts each coin from the recent past of all coins.
      Volatility
      How jumpy a price is; we measure it from recent hourly returns.
      z-score
      How many "normal moves" this hour's move is; above 2.29 counts as a shock.
